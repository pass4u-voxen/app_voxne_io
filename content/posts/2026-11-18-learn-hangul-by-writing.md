You recognize ㄱ and ㅏ when you see them, but when you sit down with a blank page, you hesitate. That's the gap between **recognizing Hangul** and being able to produce it. If you want to **learn Hangul by writing**, the goal is not to fill pages with copied characters. It's to look, write, hide the example, and retrieve the shape from memory.

This guide assumes you're new to the Korean alphabet. It gives you a short routine you can repeat, plus a way to tell whether your writing is actually becoming more independent.

## First, understand what you're writing

Hangul letters are arranged into **syllable blocks**, not written in a continuous horizontal string like English letters. Each block has an initial consonant position and a vowel, and some blocks have a final consonant called **받침** (*batchim*).

Start with these combinations:

| Pieces | Block | Approximate reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ㄴ + ㅏ | 나 | na |
| ㅁ + ㅗ | 모 | mo |
| ㄱ + ㅏ | 가 | ga |
| ㅇ + ㅣ | 이 | i |
| ㅂ + ㅏ + ㅁ | 밤 | bam |

The English-letter readings are only approximations. In particular, ㄱ and ㅂ do not have perfectly identical English equivalents in every position. **ㅇ is silent at the beginning of a syllable**, but it represents *ng* when used as a final consonant.

If arranging letters still feels unfamiliar, first review [how to write Hangul syllable blocks](/how-to-write-hangul/). Here we'll focus on building a practice habit that makes those shapes easier to recall.

## The 15-minute write–hide–recall routine

You need a pen, paper, and a reference showing the letters correctly. Squared paper can help you keep each block together, but ordinary paper works too.

### Minutes 0–3: observe five letters

Choose just five symbols for the day, such as **ㄱ, ㄴ, ㅁ, ㅏ, ㅗ**. Look at each shape and notice what distinguishes it from the others. Trace each one once slowly. Pay attention to its lines and orientation, rather than trying to draw it as fast as possible.

Write each symbol two more times while the reference is visible. Don't move on simply because the page looks full. The next stage is what tests your memory.

### Minutes 3–6: cover and write

Cover the examples. Say or think of a symbol, then write it without peeking. Reveal the reference and compare immediately.

Ask three questions: Is the shape correct? Is it facing the right direction? Could I distinguish it from another letter at a glance?

If you mix up **ㅏ** and **ㅓ**, draw them side by side and pay attention to which side the short stroke points toward. If you confuse **ㅗ** and **ㅜ**, compare the short stroke above or below the horizontal line. Make the comparison specific; writing the same wrong form ten times will not fix it.

### Minutes 6–10: assemble syllables

Now combine the letters into blocks. With **ㄴ, ㅁ, ㄱ, ㅏ, ㅗ**, you can make **나, 마, 가, 노, 모, 고**.

Notice the layout: in **나**, the vertical vowel ㅏ sits to the right of ㄴ. In **노**, the horizontal vowel ㅗ sits below ㄴ. The consonant and vowel share one imaginary square.

Write each block once from a model, then once from memory. Try changing one component: **나 → 마** changes the consonant; **나 → 노** changes the vowel. This is more useful than copying a whole word without knowing its parts.

### Minutes 10–13: write a tiny word set

Add a few familiar blocks from your reference and practice short words:

- **나무** (*namu*) — tree
- **바다** (*bada*) — sea
- **모자** (*moja*) — hat

Read the word, cover it, and rebuild each syllable. You may need to learn new letters to complete these examples; that's fine. Introduce them deliberately rather than guessing. A word you can reconstruct is a better checkpoint than a word you can only copy.

### Minutes 13–15: test without a model

On a clean part of the page, write your five target symbols and three syllables from memory. Check them against the reference. Circle mistakes, then write the corrected form once.

Keep the test page. Tomorrow, start by attempting the same items **before** looking at the answers. That delayed attempt tells you more about retention than a perfect page written while copying.

## How to avoid the most common practice traps

**Copying without recall.** Repetition can help you become familiar with a shape, but it is easy to mistake familiarity for memory. After two or three copies, hide the example.

**Learning too many letters at once.** A large chart is useful as a reference, not necessarily as a single day's assignment. Start with a small set and add more when you can produce the earlier ones.

**Writing isolated letters forever.** You also need to see how letters change their proportions inside blocks. Alternate single-letter practice with syllable construction.

**Ignoring your errors.** If you keep reversing a vowel or placing it on the wrong side, stop and compare two similar examples. Correcting a specific confusion is more valuable than increasing your page count.

**Treating romanization as the answer.** English approximations can help you get started, but they cannot capture every Korean sound. Practice recognizing and writing the actual Hangul blocks, and use Korean audio when working on pronunciation.

## A simple seven-day progression

Use the same 15-minute structure, but vary the material. On days one and two, focus on a handful of basic consonants and vowels. On days three and four, combine them into two-letter syllable blocks. On day five, introduce a few blocks with a final consonant, such as **간** and **밤**. On day six, write short familiar words. On day seven, return to your first day's symbols and test yourself before reviewing.

You don't have to master the entire alphabet in a week. The point of the schedule is to build a repeatable cycle: **see → write → hide → recall → check**.

Once you can build blocks reliably, your next challenge is that real Korean words don't always sound exactly as they look. The guide to [reading and writing Korean sound patterns](/how-to-read-and-write-korean/) explains why.

## What progress actually looks like

Progress is not the number of notebook pages you fill. It's being able to write **나** or **모** after the example is hidden, identify which component you got wrong, and correct it without starting over.

Try one small session today. Choose five symbols, make six syllables, and finish with a two-minute memory test. Save the page so tomorrow's first attempt has something real to compare against.
