You open a Korean alphabet chart and see forty-odd symbols in a grid, with no hint about where to start.

Here's the good news for anyone looking for the **Korean alphabet for beginners**: Hangul is not a random set of shapes. It was designed in the 15th century, under King Sejong, to be learnable, and most of its letters are built from a handful of basic forms. Once you see that system, the chart shrinks from forty things to memorize into about a dozen ideas.

## Learn the families, not the grid

A chart arranges letters alphabetically. Your memory works better with **relationships**. In Hangul, many consonants are a basic shape with one or two extra strokes, and most vowels are a long line with one or two short marks attached.

So instead of asking "what is this letter?", ask "what is this letter **built from**?"

The modern alphabet has **24 basic letters: 14 consonants and 10 vowels**. Doubled consonants and combined vowels bring the commonly taught total to 40, but those are built from the 24 and come later.

## The 14 basic consonants, in five families

According to the original explanation of the script, the five basic consonant shapes were modeled on the **speech organs** used to make each sound:

| Family | Basic shape | Built from it | Hint |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Back of tongue | **ㄱ** (g/k) | **ㅋ** (k) | ㄱ suggests the tongue raised to the back of the mouth. |
| Tip of tongue | **ㄴ** (n) | **ㄷ** (d/t), **ㅌ** (t), **ㄹ** (r/l) | ㄴ suggests the tongue tip touching behind the teeth. |
| Lips | **ㅁ** (m) | **ㅂ** (b/p), **ㅍ** (p) | ㅁ is a closed mouth. |
| Teeth | **ㅅ** (s) | **ㅈ** (j), **ㅊ** (ch) | ㅅ resembles a tooth. |
| Throat | **ㅇ** (silent / ng) | **ㅎ** (h) | ㅇ is an open throat. |

Notice the pattern: **an extra stroke usually means a stronger sound**. ㄱ becomes ㅋ, a breathier *k*. ㄴ gains a top line to become ㄷ, and another line to become ㅌ. ㅈ gains a small mark on top to become ㅊ. ㄹ is the one exception that doesn't follow the add-a-stroke logic neatly, so give it a little extra attention.

Two details trip up most beginners:

1. **ㅇ has two jobs.** At the start of a syllable it is silent (아 is just *a*). At the end of a syllable it sounds like *ng* (방 is *bang*).
2. **Romanization is approximate.** ㄱ is written *g* before a vowel and *k* at the end of a syllable in the official Revised Romanization, and the real sound sits somewhere between English g and k. Use native audio for every letter; the Latin letters are only a reminder.

## The 10 basic vowels: three strokes and a rule

The vowels are even more systematic. They come from three elements: a horizontal line (**ㅡ**, *eu*), a vertical line (**ㅣ**, *i*), and a short mark that was originally a dot.

1. **Attach one short mark** to get four vowels: **ㅏ** (a), **ㅓ** (eo), **ㅗ** (o), **ㅜ** (u).
2. **Double the short mark** to add a *y* sound: **ㅑ** (ya), **ㅕ** (yeo), **ㅛ** (yo), **ㅠ** (yu).
3. Add the two plain lines, **ㅡ** (eu) and **ㅣ** (i), and you have all ten.

That one rule, *two marks = add y*, saves you from memorizing four letters separately. The position of the mark also tells you which way the vowel points: ㅏ points right, ㅓ left, ㅗ up, ㅜ down.

The other common vowels are combinations you can read once you know these ten. For example, **ㅐ** (ae) and **ㅔ** (e) look like ㅏ and ㅓ with an extra line, and **ㅘ** (wa) is literally ㅗ + ㅏ. Many speakers today pronounce ㅐ and ㅔ almost the same, so don't worry if you can't hear a difference yet.

## How letters become syllables

Korean isn't written letter by letter in a row. Letters are grouped into **syllable blocks**, each starting with a consonant (ㅇ if the syllable begins with a vowel sound).

- A vertical vowel goes to the **right**: ㄴ + ㅏ = **나** (na).
- A horizontal vowel goes **underneath**: ㄴ + ㅗ = **노** (no).
- A final consonant, called **batchim**, goes at the **bottom**: ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ = **한** (han).

That's why it pays to learn vowels by orientation. The moment you know whether a vowel is tall or flat, you know where it sits in the block. For the movement of each letter and how components share the square, see this guide to [Hangul stroke order](/hangul-stroke-order/).

## A sensible order to learn the alphabet

Rather than going down the chart, try this sequence over a week or two, at your own pace:

1. **Six vowels first:** ㅏ, ㅓ, ㅗ, ㅜ, ㅡ, ㅣ. They let you build blocks immediately.
2. **The five basic consonant shapes:** ㄱ, ㄴ, ㅁ, ㅅ, ㅇ. Now you can write 아, 나, 고, 무, 소.
3. **The stroke-added consonants**, one family at a time: ㅋ; ㄷ, ㅌ; ㅂ, ㅍ; ㅈ, ㅊ; ㅎ. Then ㄹ on its own.
4. **The four y-vowels:** ㅑ, ㅕ, ㅛ, ㅠ, using the two-mark rule.
5. **Doubled consonants and combined vowels** (ㄲ, ㄸ, ㅃ, ㅆ, ㅉ; ㅐ, ㅔ, ㅘ, ㅝ and the rest) once the basics feel easy.

At each step, **write the letters by hand** rather than only reading them. Recognizing a shape on a chart is easier than producing it, and trying to write a letter from memory shows you immediately which ones haven't stuck. Paper and a chart work fine; so does a handwriting app with stroke-order animations and audio, if that's easier to open every day. If you'd like a daily routine built around that write-and-recall loop, this guide on [learning Korean by writing](/how-to-learn-korean-by-writing/) lays one out.

## Common beginner mistakes

**Trying to learn all 40 at once.** Learn the 24 basic letters first; the rest are combinations.

**Trusting romanization over sound.** *eo* and *eu* look strange in Latin letters because English has no exact equivalents. Listen and imitate.

**Confusing ㅗ and ㅜ, or ㅏ and ㅓ.** Say the direction aloud as you write: "mark up, *o*; mark down, *u*."

## Try this today

Take a sheet of paper and write the six first vowels: **ㅏ ㅓ ㅗ ㅜ ㅡ ㅣ**. Put a silent ㅇ in front of each to make **아 어 오 우 으 이**, and say each one aloud after listening to a recording. Cover the page and write all six from memory.

That's your first six real Korean syllables, and the foundation the rest of the alphabet builds on.
