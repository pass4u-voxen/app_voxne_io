Writing your own name is usually the first thing anyone wants to do in a new script. It's personal, it's short, and it's the word you'll use most. In Korean it's also a surprisingly good lesson, because getting it right forces you to understand how Hangul actually works.

The catch: online converters often hand you a spelling that looks fine but sounds off, or give three different answers for the same name. Once you know the handful of rules behind them, you can work out your name yourself and know why it's spelled that way.

## The key idea: Korean spells sounds, not letters

Hangul doesn't transliterate English letter by letter. It writes **how your name sounds**. "Sophia" and "Sofia" come out the same (소피아), and the silent letters in "Hannah" or "Michael" simply disappear.

So the first step is not to look at your name, but to say it slowly and split it into sounds: *Mi-chael* is really "my-kul", *Emily* is "e-mi-li". Then you map each sound to Korean.

Two structural facts shape everything else:

1. **Korean writes in syllable blocks.** Each block is a consonant + vowel, sometimes with a final consonant (called **batchim**) underneath. 마 is ㅁ + ㅏ; 클 is ㅋ + ㅡ + ㄹ.
2. **Every block starts with a consonant.** If a syllable begins with a vowel sound, you use **ㅇ** as a silent placeholder. That's why "Emily" starts with 에 (ㅇ + ㅔ) and "Olivia" with 올.

## How common English sounds become Hangul

These are the conversions that trip people up most. They follow the general pattern of Korea's official loanword rules, with notes where everyday spelling differs.

1. **R and L both become ㄹ.** Korean has one letter covering both. *Laura* → 로라, *Ryan* → 라이언, *Paul* → 폴.
2. **An L between vowels is usually doubled.** *Emily* → 에밀리, *Olivia* → 올리비아. The first ㄹ sits as batchim, the second starts the next block. This keeps the "l" sound from turning into a flapped "r".
3. **F becomes ㅍ.** There's no F in Korean, so *Sophia* → 소피아, *Jennifer* → 제니퍼.
4. **V becomes ㅂ.** *Victoria* → 빅토리아, *David* → 데이비드.
5. **Z becomes ㅈ, and TH usually becomes ㅅ.** *Zoe* → 조이, *Elizabeth* → 엘리자베스.
6. **"Ay" and "eye" sounds split into two vowels.** The "ay" in *Jane* is 에이 (제인), the "i" in *Mike* is 아이 (마이크).
7. **The "a" in "cat" is ㅐ.** *Sam* → 샘, *Adam* → 애덤, *Daniel* → 대니얼.
8. **Consonant clusters and final sounds get a ㅡ vowel.** Korean can't stack consonants, so each extra one gets the neutral vowel ㅡ: *Chris* → 크리스, *Grace* → 그레이스, *Mark* → 마크, *James* → 제임스.
9. **Final N, M, L and NG stay as batchim.** *John* → 존, *Sam* → 샘, *Paul* → 폴. In loanwords only seven consonants are used as finals (ㄱ ㄴ ㄹ ㅁ ㅂ ㅅ ㅇ), and for names you'll mostly see ㄴ, ㄹ, ㅁ and ㅇ.

### When two spellings both exist

Official spellings and popular ones sometimes disagree. *Daniel* is 대니얼 by the rules but very often written 다니엘; *Sarah* is 세라 officially and 사라 in everyday use. Neither is "wrong" in casual life, and any Korean speaker will recognize both. If you need one fixed spelling, for a visa form, a signature or a tattoo, pick one and use it consistently. A good sanity check is to see how a well-known person with your name is spelled in Korean media.

## Worked examples, block by block

**Michael** (*my-kul*)
- 마 (ㅁ + ㅏ) = "ma"
- 이 (ㅇ + ㅣ) = "i", completing the "my" sound
- 클 (ㅋ + ㅡ + ㄹ) = "kul"
- Result: **마이클**

**Olivia** (*o-li-vi-a*)
- 올 (ㅇ + ㅗ + ㄹ): vowel start needs ㅇ, and the first L of the doubled pair goes underneath
- 리 (ㄹ + ㅣ), 비 (ㅂ + ㅣ, since V becomes ㅂ), 아 (ㅇ + ㅏ)
- Result: **올리비아**

**Grace** (*grays*)
- 그 (ㄱ + ㅡ): the G of the cluster gets ㅡ
- 레 (ㄹ + ㅔ) + 이 (ㅇ + ㅣ) for the "ay"
- 스 (ㅅ + ㅡ) for the final S
- Result: **그레이스**

Notice how a one-syllable English name can become three or four Korean blocks. That's normal, and it's why Korean versions of names often look longer than you expect.

## Writing it by hand

Knowing the spelling is half the job. To write it so it looks natural:

- **Follow the basic stroke order:** top to bottom, left to right, and horizontal strokes before the vertical strokes that cross them. If you want the full rules, see this guide to [Hangul stroke order](/hangul-stroke-order/).
- **Pay attention to block shape.** With a vertical vowel like ㅏ or ㅣ, the consonant sits on the left (마, 이). With a horizontal vowel like ㅗ or ㅡ, it sits on top (로, 그). A batchim goes along the bottom (클, 존). Each block should fill roughly the same square, so letters shrink when the block is crowded.
- **Draw ㅇ as one stroke**, starting at the top and going counterclockwise. **ㅁ takes three strokes:** the left side, then the top and right side in one motion, then the bottom.

Most beginners write their name a few times, then can't reproduce it a day later. Copying feels like learning, but the real test is writing it **from memory**. Writing by hand, with attention to each stroke, is what tends to make it stick. Practice sheets with a square grid, or a handwriting app that animates stroke order, help with proportions while your eye adjusts. If you're still learning the letters themselves, a [Korean alphabet guide for beginners](/korean-alphabet-for-beginners/) is the better place to start.

## Try it today

Say your name out loud slowly and split it into syllables. Map each sound with the list above, build the blocks on paper, then write the full name five times. Cover your notes and write it once more from memory. If you can do that tomorrow morning, you'll have learned your first Korean word and most of the rules you'll need for the next hundred.
