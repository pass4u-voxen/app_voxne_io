Katakana looks like it should be easy to write. Most characters are two or three short, straight strokes, and nothing loops. Yet it's exactly that simplicity that causes trouble: with so little on the page, the *order and direction* of each stroke is most of what makes one character different from another.

The good news is that **katakana stroke order** follows a small set of rules. Learn those, plus a dozen characters that bend them or get confused, and the rest takes care of itself.

## Why order matters more in katakana than you'd expect

In hiragana, a wrong stroke order mostly makes a character look a bit off. In katakana it can turn one character into another. シ (*shi*) and ツ (*tsu*), ン (*n*) and ソ (*so*), ク (*ku*) and ケ (*ke*): the strokes are almost the same, and the difference lives in where each one starts and which way it travels.

Katakana also came from pieces of kanji (カ from 加, ロ from 口), and its stroke order follows the same tradition, so getting it right now is a head start on kanji later.

If you've already learned [hiragana stroke order](/hiragana-stroke-order/), you know most of the principles. Katakana uses the same ones, just with straighter lines.

## The rules that cover almost every character

1. **Top to bottom.** ニ, ミ and ヨ build downward, one horizontal at a time.
2. **Left to right.** In ハ, リ and ル, the left stroke comes first.
3. **Horizontal before a vertical that crosses it.** キ, サ, オ and ホ all start with the horizontal.
4. **A left-falling stroke (ノ) usually comes before what it attaches to.** ク, タ, ケ, チ, メ and イ all open with a short ノ-shaped stroke from upper right to lower left.
5. **Small dots and finishing touches come last.** The dot inside タ, the dot at the end of ム, the two small marks at the bottom of ホ.
6. **Watch how each stroke ends.** Strokes end in a **stop** (*tome*), a small **flick** (*hane*) or a tapering **sweep** (*harai*). The first stroke of カ ends with a flick at the bottom of the hook. ノ sweeps away to a point. レ finishes with a flick up to the right.

Single-stroke characters like ノ, ヘ, フ and レ have no order to get wrong, only a direction.

## The characters beginners get wrong most often

Stroke counts follow the standard school forms.

1. **シ, ツ, ン, ソ.** The four most famous mix-ups. In シ and ン, the long final stroke **rises** from the lower left. In ツ and ソ, it **falls** from the upper right. The short strokes are written first in all four. There's a fuller look at these pairs in [katakana vs hiragana](/katakana-vs-hiragana/).
2. **カ (ka), 2 strokes.** The horizontal-and-hook comes first, then the slanted stroke through it. Many learners start with the slant, as if writing the left half of a box.
3. **ク, タ, ケ (ku, ta, ke).** All three begin with the same short ノ. ク adds one stroke that runs across and sweeps down. タ adds that same stroke, then a small dot inside. ケ is different: after the ノ comes a short horizontal, then a long sweep that starts from the middle of that horizontal.
4. **ナ (na) and メ (me), 2 strokes each.** Both are two crossing strokes, which is why they get mixed up. ナ starts with the **horizontal**, then the long stroke down to the left. メ starts with the long **ノ**, then a short stroke crossing it toward the lower right.
5. **ス (su) and ヌ (nu), 2 strokes each.** Both start with the same フ-shaped stroke. In ス, the second stroke branches off toward the lower right. In ヌ, it cuts *across* the first stroke.
6. **コ, ユ, ヨ, ロ (ko, yu, yo, ro).** コ is a horizontal turning down, then the bottom line. ユ is the same idea, but the bottom line is longer and extends past the corner. ヨ adds a middle line between the two. ロ starts with the **left vertical**, then the top-and-right stroke, then the bottom, the same order as the kanji 口.
7. **チ (chi) and テ (te), 3 strokes each.** チ opens with a short ノ, then the horizontal, then the vertical that sweeps left. テ opens with two horizontals, short then long, then the same kind of falling stroke.
8. **モ (mo), 3 strokes.** Two horizontals first, then the vertical that curves to the right. That's the opposite of hiragana も, where the vertical comes first, so this one catches people who learned hiragana well.
9. **ヤ (ya) and セ (se), 2 strokes each.** Both start with a horizontal that turns down at the right with a small hook. ヤ then adds a long slanted stroke crossing it. セ adds a vertical that curves along the bottom to the right.
10. **ネ (ne), 4 strokes.** Dot, then the stroke that goes across and down to the left, then the vertical, then the small mark on the right.

One more detail: the **long-vowel mark ー** is a single stroke written left to right in horizontal text and top to bottom in vertical text. Small characters like ッ, ャ and ュ use exactly the same order as their full-size versions.

## How to practice without turning it into a chore

Stroke order sticks through doing, with a check at the end, not through looking at charts.

1. **Watch the stroke move once,** then write it slowly three times, saying the sound and counting strokes: "ka: one, two."
2. **Practice in contrast groups.** シ/ツ/ン/ソ together, ナ/メ together, ク/タ/ケ together, コ/ユ/ヨ/ロ together. Writing them side by side forces you to feel the difference.
3. **Cover the model and write from memory.** This is the real test. If you hesitate over which stroke comes first, you haven't got it yet.
4. **Check order and endings, not just shape.**
5. **Use real words.** Loanwords are the natural home of katakana: write メニュー (*menyū*, menu), コーヒー (*kōhī*, coffee), タクシー (*takushī*, taxi). You practice stroke order, the long-vowel mark and small characters at once.

Squared notebook paper keeps proportions even. If you'd rather practice on a screen, a handwriting app that animates each stroke and lets you trace over it gives you the watch-then-write loop quickly. Either way, the learning happens in your hand.

Five to ten minutes a day on one contrast group is plenty.

## A small step for today

Take a pen and write four close relatives in a line: ク, タ, ケ and カ. Write each one five times, saying the stroke count as you go. Then cover your page and write メニュー from memory. If the ノ comes first in every place it should, you're ready for the next group tomorrow.
