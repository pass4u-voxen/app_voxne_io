You've learned the letters. You can sound out 가, 나, 다 without hesitating. Then you try to read a real word, like 한국어 or 감사합니다, and what comes out of your mouth doesn't match what you hear from a native speaker. Or you listen to a word, write it the way it sounds, and get the spelling wrong.

Nothing is wrong with your ear. **Reading and writing Korean** are two directions across the same small gap: Korean spelling shows what a word *is*, and pronunciation shows how it's *said* when it sits next to other sounds. That gap is covered by a handful of regular rules. Learn them, and both reading and writing start to line up.

## Why spelling and sound drift apart

Hangul is very close to phonetic, but it isn't a pure sound recording. Korean spelling keeps each word part looking the same wherever it appears. Take **옷** (*ot*, "clothes"):

- 옷 on its own sounds like *ot*.
- 옷이 ("clothes" + subject marker) sounds like *osi*.
- 옷도 ("clothes too") sounds like *ot-tto*.

Three pronunciations, one spelling. That's a feature, not a flaw: once you can read, you recognize 옷 instantly in every form. But it means reading is "spelling → sound" and writing is "sound → spelling," and each direction needs the rules applied the right way round.

If you're still unsure how letters stack into a block, start with [how to write Hangul syllable blocks](/how-to-write-hangul/) and come back. Everything below assumes you can decode a single block.

## Reading a single block

Read every block in the same order: **top-left, then right or below, then the bottom.**

- 한 = ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ → *han*
- 국 = ㄱ + ㅜ + ㄱ → *guk*
- 아 = ㅇ + ㅏ → *a* (ㅇ at the start of a block is silent)
- 강 = ㄱ + ㅏ + ㅇ → *gang* (ㅇ at the bottom is *ng*)

That last pair trips up many beginners: **ㅇ is silent at the top and pronounced *ng* at the bottom.**

## Five sound rules that cover most surprises

These five rules explain most of the moments where a word "doesn't sound like it's spelled." Learn them in this order; each is common enough that you'll meet it in your first week of real words.

### 1. Final consonants collapse to seven sounds

At the bottom of a block, Korean only allows seven sounds: **ㄱ, ㄴ, ㄷ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅂ, ㅇ**. Every other final consonant folds into one of them:

- ㅋ, ㄲ → ㄱ: 부엌 sounds like *bu-eok*, 밖 like *bak*
- ㅅ, ㅆ, ㅈ, ㅊ, ㅌ, ㅎ → ㄷ: 옷 is *ot*, 낮 is *nat*
- ㅍ → ㅂ: 앞 is *ap*

So 낫, 낮 and 낟 all sound the same on their own. Only the spelling tells them apart.

### 2. Linking: a final consonant slides into a silent ㅇ

When a block ends in a consonant and the next block starts with silent ㅇ, the consonant moves over and is pronounced at the start of the next syllable.

- 음악 → [으막] *eumak* ("music")
- 한국어 → [한구거] *hangugeo* ("Korean language")
- 옷이 → [오시] *osi*

This is why fluent Korean sounds so connected. When reading, look ahead one block: if it starts with ㅇ, carry the consonant across.

### 3. Nasalization: hard finals soften before ㄴ and ㅁ

When a final ㄱ, ㄷ or ㅂ sound meets a following ㄴ or ㅁ, it turns into the matching nasal: **ㄱ → ㅇ, ㄷ → ㄴ, ㅂ → ㅁ.**

- 감사합니다 → [감사함니다] *gamsahamnida* ("thank you")
- 국물 → [궁물] *gungmul* ("broth")
- 한국말 → [한궁말] *hangungmal* ("Korean speech")

You'll hear this in almost every polite sentence, because so many end in -ㅂ니다.

### 4. ㄴ and ㄹ side by side both become ㄹ

When ㄴ and ㄹ meet, in either order, both are usually pronounced as ㄹ (an *l* sound):

- 신라 → [실라] *Silla* (the ancient kingdom)
- 설날 → [설랄] *Seollal* (Lunar New Year)

### 5. ㅎ makes its neighbor breathy

ㅎ next to ㄱ, ㄷ or ㅈ merges with it into the aspirated version: ㅋ, ㅌ or ㅊ.

- 좋다 → [조타] *jota* ("to be good")
- 축하 → [추카] *chuka* ("congratulations")

There are more rules (tensing, as in 학교 sounding like *hak-kkyo*, is the next one worth learning), but these five already make most beginner texts readable aloud.

## Writing: go from sound back to spelling

When you write, run the rules in reverse. You hear [함니다], but you write **합니다**. You hear [한구거], but you write **한국어**. The question to ask is not "what did I hear?" but "**which word parts is this made of?**"

Three habits make that easier:

1. **Learn words in their written form first.** If you only ever hear 감사합니다, you'll spell it the way it sounds. Seeing the spelling early anchors it.
2. **Notice the stable piece.** 옷, 옷이, 옷도 all share 옷. Once you know the core word, the endings attach to it predictably.
3. **Write from memory, not from a copy.** Copying a word while looking at it trains your hand. Writing it after hearing it trains the link between sound and spelling, which is the part that actually fails in dictation.

Writing by hand is especially useful here. Forming each block forces you to decide where every consonant goes, which is exactly the decision the sound rules blur. It's the same reason the [stroke order](/hangul-stroke-order/) matters early: the hand remembers structure that the eye skims over.

## A daily routine for both directions

Ten to fifteen minutes is enough:

1. **Read aloud (5 min).** Take five to eight words from a menu, a sign, or a short text. Before saying each one, mark where a rule applies: an arrow for linking, a circle for nasalization.
2. **Listen and write (5 min).** Play audio of the same words (a dictionary app, a textbook recording, or a handwriting app with audio and write-from-memory drills). Write each one without looking, then check the spelling.
3. **Fix one rule (2–3 min).** Look at your mistakes. They'll usually cluster around one rule. Write three more words that use it.

Keep the word list small and real. Ten words you can both read and spell correctly are worth more than fifty you can only recognize.

## Try this today

Take these five words: **한국어, 음악, 감사합니다, 옷이, 좋다.** Read each one aloud and name the rule that changes its sound. Then cover the list, say each word out loud, and write it from memory. If your spelling matches the original all five times, you're already reading and writing Korean in both directions.
