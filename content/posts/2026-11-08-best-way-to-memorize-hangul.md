You've looked at the Hangul chart a dozen times. Each time it makes sense, and each time, a day later, you're squinting at **ㅓ** and **ㅗ** trying to remember which is which. Looking at the chart feels like studying, so why doesn't it stick?

Because recognizing a letter while the answer sits next to it is a different skill from **producing it from memory**. The **best way to memorize Hangul** is to stop rereading and start retrieving: write letters with the chart hidden, read real words with no romanization, and spend extra time on the handful of letters you confuse. The alphabet is small enough that this works quickly, but the method matters more than the hours.

## Why rereading the chart doesn't work

A chart gives you the answer before you've had to look for it. Your brain registers "yes, I know that one," and the feeling of familiarity is easy to mistake for knowing.

Memory gets stronger when you **pull information out**, not when you put it in again. Every time you try to recall what **ㅂ** sounds like, struggle a little, and then check, you strengthen the path you'll need when you see ㅂ in a real word. Getting it wrong and correcting it is part of the process, not a sign it isn't working.

Three habits follow from that:

1. **Hide the answer first.** Cover the chart, the romanization or the audio, try, then check.
2. **Space it out.** Ten minutes a day for a week beats one long session, because each day forces you to recall what has started to fade.
3. **Produce, don't just recognize.** Writing a letter from memory is harder than picking it out of a lineup, which is exactly why it helps.

## Use the system, then test yourself on it

Hangul was designed to be learnable, and you should let its logic do some of the memorizing for you. Consonants come in **families** built from a few basic shapes (ㄱ → ㅋ, ㄴ → ㄷ → ㅌ, ㅁ → ㅂ → ㅍ, ㅅ → ㅈ → ㅊ, ㅇ → ㅎ), and an extra stroke usually means a stronger or breathier sound. Vowels are a long line plus short marks, and a **doubled mark adds a *y* sound**: ㅏ (a) becomes ㅑ (ya). If you haven't seen this layout yet, the [guide to the Korean alphabet for beginners](/korean-alphabet-for-beginners/) walks through every family.

But understanding the system is still input. Turn it into recall questions:

- "Which letter is ㄴ plus one line?" (ㄷ)
- "Add a *y* to ㅜ (u). What do you write?" (ㅠ, yu)
- "Which family does ㅍ belong to?" (the lips: ㅁ, ㅂ, ㅍ)

Answer out loud or on paper, then check. A sound-and-shape link you've had to rebuild yourself holds much better than one you've only read.

## Target the pairs you confuse

Most beginners don't forget the whole alphabet. They mix up a few specific pairs, and those deserve their own practice.

| Pair | How to tell them apart |
| --- | --- |
| **ㅏ (a) / ㅓ (eo)** | The short mark points away from the consonant in ㅏ (나), toward it in ㅓ (너). |
| **ㅗ (o) / ㅜ (u)** | The mark points up in ㅗ (오), down in ㅜ (우). |
| **ㅓ (eo) / ㅕ (yeo)** | One mark versus two; two marks add *y*. |
| **ㅁ (m) / ㅂ (b)** | ㅂ has two upright lines that stick up above its middle bar; ㅁ is a closed box. |
| **ㄷ (d) / ㅌ (t)** | ㅌ has one extra horizontal line, and a breathier sound. |
| **ㅈ (j) / ㅊ (ch)** | ㅊ adds a small stroke on top, and a breathier sound. |

A simple drill: write each pair side by side five times, saying the sound as you write. Then have someone read random syllables (or play random audio) and write what you hear, such as 너, 노, 누, 나. **Dictation** is the hardest test of all, because you have to go from sound to shape with no visual hint.

## Read real words as early as possible

Single letters are abstract. Words give you feedback. **Loanwords from English** are especially good, because when you decode one correctly you recognize it instantly:

1. **바나나** (banana) — banana
2. **커피** (keopi) — coffee
3. **버스** (beoseu) — bus
4. **피자** (pija) — pizza
5. **카메라** (kamera) — camera
6. **택시** (taeksi) — taxi

Cover the romanization and sound each one out block by block. If what you say turns into a word you know, you've checked yourself without needing an answer key. Then move to native words such as **나무** (namu, tree), **우유** (uyu, milk) and **이름** (ireum, name), where the meaning won't give the answer away.

Remember that romanization is only a rough hint. Listen to native audio for each letter, especially vowels like ㅓ and ㅡ, which have no exact English match.

## Why writing by hand helps

Writing a letter from memory forces you to recall every part of it: how many strokes, where the short mark goes, which way it points. You can't half-remember a letter and still write it correctly. That is why handwriting practice tends to show you which letters you really know, and it makes an easy daily habit. Writing in the correct order also keeps the shapes consistent; the [Hangul stroke order guide](/hangul-stroke-order/) covers the basic movements.

A pen and squared paper are enough. If you'd rather practice on a phone, look for a **handwriting tool that asks you to write from memory** and checks the result, not one that only lets you trace over a faint letter. Tracing feels productive, but the outline does the remembering for you.

## A seven-day plan

About ten to fifteen minutes a day:

1. **Day 1:** Basic vowels ㅏ ㅓ ㅗ ㅜ ㅡ ㅣ. Write each, then write them from memory.
2. **Day 2:** Recall yesterday's vowels first. Add ㄱ ㄴ ㅁ ㅅ ㅇ and build blocks such as 나, 무, 소, 이.
3. **Day 3:** Recall days 1–2. Add the rest of the consonant families.
4. **Day 4:** Recall everything. Add the *y* vowels ㅑ ㅕ ㅛ ㅠ.
5. **Day 5:** Confusable-pairs drill and dictation.
6. **Day 6:** Decode the loanwords above, then a few native words.
7. **Day 7:** Write the whole basic alphabet from memory. Circle the letters you missed; they're tomorrow's warm-up.

Expect gaps on day 7. They show you exactly what to review next, which is the point of testing yourself.

## Try this today

Close the chart and write the six basic vowels from memory: **ㅏ ㅓ ㅗ ㅜ ㅡ ㅣ**. Say each sound as you write it. Then check. Whatever you got wrong, write three more times, and test yourself again tomorrow before looking at anything.
