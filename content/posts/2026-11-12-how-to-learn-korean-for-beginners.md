You've decided to learn Korean, maybe because of a show, a trip, a friend, or music you can't stop playing. Then you open a search page and get a hundred apps, textbooks and "learn Korean fast" videos, all starting in different places.

If you're wondering **how to learn Korean for beginners**, the useful answer isn't a magic resource. It's an **order**. Korean has a few foundations that make everything after them easier, and a few traps that waste beginners' first months. Get the order right and any decent textbook, app or class becomes far more effective.

## The insight: Korean is hard in a different place than you expect

Most people assume the alphabet will be the hard part. It's the opposite. **Hangul** is one of the most learnable writing systems in the world: 24 basic letters, built on a visible logic. Many learners can read it slowly within a week or two of steady practice.

What takes real time is everything after the alphabet: **sounds that change** when letters meet, a **sentence order** that puts the verb last, small **particles** that mark each word's role, and **speech levels** that change verb endings depending on who you're talking to. A good beginner plan gets the alphabet done quickly and properly, then spends its energy on those four things.

## Month 1: Hangul and the sound system

### 1. Learn the alphabet by writing it

Start with the 14 basic consonants and 10 basic vowels, grouped by shape rather than memorized as a chart. This guide to the [Korean alphabet for beginners](/korean-alphabet-for-beginners/) explains the letter families. Write each letter by hand and try to produce it from memory, not just recognize it. Producing a letter is harder than recognizing one, and that difficulty is what makes it stick.

### 2. Drop romanization as early as you can

Romanization (writing Korean in Latin letters, like *annyeonghaseyo*) is a crutch that turns into a trap. It can't represent sounds like **ㅓ** (*eo*) or **ㅡ** (*eu*) accurately, and it makes you read Korean through English habits. Use it for the first few days, then switch to Hangul plus audio.

### 3. Learn the two sound rules that matter most

- **Final consonants simplify.** A consonant at the bottom of a syllable block (*batchim*) is pronounced as one of only seven sounds: ㄱ, ㄴ, ㄷ, ㄹ, ㅁ, ㅂ, ㅇ. So 옷 (clothes) ends in a *t* sound, not *s*.
- **Final consonants carry over.** When a block ending in a consonant is followed by one starting with silent ㅇ, the consonant moves forward. **한국어** (Korean language) is written *han-guk-eo* but sounds like *han-gu-geo*.

These two rules explain a large share of the "it doesn't sound like it's written" moments beginners hit.

## Month 2: Sentence structure and your first grammar

### 4. Get used to the verb coming last

Korean is a **subject–object–verb** language. Where English says "I drink coffee", Korean says "I coffee drink":

- **저는 커피를 마셔요.** (*jeoneun keopireul masyeoyo*) — I drink coffee.

Once you accept that the verb waits until the end, longer sentences become much less intimidating. You can often ignore the middle, find the verb, and know what's happening.

### 5. Learn the core particles

Particles are short endings attached to nouns that tell you each word's job. Because of them, Korean word order is more flexible than English, and you can't skip them. Start with four, each of which has a form after a consonant and a form after a vowel:

1. **은 / 는** — topic ("as for…"): 저**는** (as for me)
2. **이 / 가** — subject: 물**이** (water, as subject)
3. **을 / 를** — object: 커피**를** (coffee, as object)
4. **에** — destination or time: 학교**에** 가요 (I go to school)

Don't try to master the difference between 은/는 and 이/가 in month two. Native-like use takes a long time. Notice it, and keep going.

### 6. Pick one speech level and use it everywhere

Korean verbs change their endings depending on politeness. For a beginner, the most useful is the **polite -요 form** (해요체), which is appropriate with strangers, shopkeepers and most adults you don't know well:

- 가다 (to go) → **가요**
- 먹다 (to eat) → **먹어요** (*meogeoyo*)
- 하다 (to do) → **해요**

Learn this one style first. Formal endings like 감사합니다 (*gamsahamnida*) and casual speech with close friends can come later. You'll still recognize them; you just don't need to produce them yet.

## Month 3: Vocabulary, listening and real use

### 7. Learn words that fit the sentences you can already make

Vocabulary sticks better when it plugs into a pattern. With **X 주세요** (*X juseyo*, "X, please"), every food or object you learn becomes a usable sentence: 물 주세요 (water, please). With **저는 X이에요 / 예요** ("I am X"), every job or nationality does too: 저는 학생이에요 (I'm a student).

### 8. Learn the two number systems separately

Korean has **native numbers** (하나, 둘, 셋…) and **Sino-Korean numbers** (일, 이, 삼…). Roughly, native numbers count things and hours; Sino-Korean numbers handle minutes, dates, money and phone numbers. Learn one system at a time, then practice telling the time, which uses both.

### 9. Listen to slow, clear Korean every day

Beginner podcasts, graded listening and slowed-down dialogues beat dramas at this stage. Dramas are great motivation, but at normal speed they're mostly noise to a beginner. Use them for fun, and use slow audio for learning.

## Making it a habit

A realistic beginner routine is **15–25 minutes a day**, split roughly like this:

- **5 minutes** writing: a few letters, syllables or words from memory.
- **10 minutes** on one grammar point or sentence pattern, with examples you write yourself.
- **5–10 minutes** of listening, repeating short lines aloud.

Daily and short works better than one long weekend session, because spaced review is what keeps things from fading. If paper feels like a chore, a handwriting app with stroke-order animations and audio can make the writing part easier to open each day. For a step-by-step version of that writing routine, see [how to learn Korean by writing](/how-to-learn-korean-by-writing/).

Progress in Korean is uneven. Reading comes quickly, speaking slowly, and listening seems stuck for weeks before it suddenly improves. That's normal, not a sign you're doing it wrong.

## Try this today

Write this sentence by hand, say it aloud, then cover it and write it again from memory:

**저는 커피를 마셔요.**

Circle the two particles (는, 를) and underline the verb at the end. You've just used Korean word order, two particles and the polite -요 ending, which are the three ideas that most of your first three months will build on.
