You want to start Korean, and you've hit the first fork in the road. Some resources open with phrases in Latin letters, like *annyeonghaseyo*, so you can "start speaking on day one." Others insist you sit down and learn Hangul before anything else. If you only want to talk, the alphabet can feel like a detour.

It isn't. For almost everyone, the right call is to **learn the Korean alphabet first**, and the reason isn't tradition or strictness. It's that Hangul is small, logical and quick to learn, while the alternative quietly costs you months.

## Why "alphabet first" is the shortcut, not the long way

With many languages, "learn the script first" really is a big ask. Japanese has two syllabaries and thousands of kanji. Chinese has no alphabet at all. Korean is different. **Hangul** was designed in the fifteenth century to be easy to learn, and it shows: 14 basic consonants, 10 basic vowels, and a few predictable combinations built from them. Many learners can read slowly within a week or two of steady daily practice.

So the real comparison isn't "a short path vs. a long one." It's a short investment now vs. a slow tax later. Here's what that tax looks like.

### 1. Romanization can't hold Korean sounds

Latin letters were never built for Korean, so romanization has to fake it. The vowels **어** and **오** are written *eo* and *o*, but an English reader sees *eo* and says something like "ee-oh," which is wrong. The consonants **ㄱ**, **ㅋ** and **ㄲ** are three different sounds, written *g/k*, *k* and *kk*, and the difference between them is exactly what romanization blurs. Every time you read romanized Korean, you rehearse an English-flavored version of the sound.

### 2. There isn't even one romanization

South Korea's official system is the Revised Romanization, but older systems and personal spellings are everywhere. The common surname **이** appears as *Lee*, *Yi* and *Rhee*. **박** shows up as *Park*, *Pak* and *Bak*. If you learn words through romanization, you're learning someone's spelling choice, not the word.

### 3. Real Korean is written in Hangul

Textbooks past the first chapter, dictionaries, subtitles, menus, song lyrics, and every sign you'll see in Korea use Hangul. Learners who postpone the alphabet usually have to stop a few weeks in and learn it anyway, now with a pile of mispronounced words to unlearn.

### 4. The alphabet teaches you pronunciation

Hangul's letters map closely to sounds, and the consonant shapes even group related sounds into families: **ㄱ → ㅋ → ㄲ**, **ㄷ → ㅌ → ㄸ**, **ㅂ → ㅍ → ㅃ**. Learning to read them is also learning to hear the difference. You get a pronunciation course built into the writing.

## What "first" should actually mean

Learning the alphabet first doesn't mean spending a month on letters before you're allowed to say hello. It means making Hangul your **main focus for the first one to three weeks**, with a few things alongside it:

1. **Basic letters before the rest.** Start with the 14 consonants and 10 vowels. Grouping them by shape, as in this guide to the [Korean alphabet for beginners](/korean-alphabet-for-beginners/), is faster than memorizing a grid.
2. **Syllable blocks from the start.** Korean letters are stacked into blocks, one per syllable: **ㄱ + ㅏ = 가**, **ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ = 한**. Practice building and reading blocks as soon as you know a handful of letters, not after you've finished the chart.
3. **Audio with every letter.** Listen and repeat. The goal is to connect each shape to a sound you've actually heard, not to a Latin letter.
4. **A few greetings, written in Hangul.** You can still learn **안녕하세요** (hello) and **감사합니다** (thank you) in week one. Just learn them as Hangul words with audio, not as romanized strings.
5. **Double consonants and compound vowels last.** **ㄲ ㄸ ㅃ ㅆ ㅉ** and vowels like **ㅐ, ㅔ, ㅘ, ㅝ** come once the basics are solid. They're mostly combinations of letters you already know.

## Practice that actually makes it stick

Reading a chart over and over feels productive but fades quickly. What makes letters stay is **producing** them: writing a letter or word from memory, checking it, and trying again.

- **Write, cover, recall.** Write a block like **나**, cover it, say it aloud, and write it again without looking.
- **Read loanwords.** English borrowings are the best early reading practice, because you already know the meaning: **커피** (coffee), **버스** (bus), **택시** (taxi), **컴퓨터** (computer). Sounding one out and recognizing it is a small, real win.
- **Keep sessions short and daily.** Ten or fifteen minutes a day beats an hour on Sunday, because each session forces you to recall what you'd half-forgotten.

If paper practice feels like a chore, a handwriting app that shows stroke order and plays each sound can make the daily writing easier to keep up. Whatever the tool, the principle is the same: **write it from memory, don't just look at it.** There's more on this in [the best way to memorize Hangul](/best-way-to-memorize-hangul/).

## How to know you're ready to move on

You don't need perfection. You're ready to shift focus to vocabulary and grammar when you can:

1. **Read any syllable block** you haven't seen before, even if slowly.
2. **Write the 24 basic letters from memory**, in roughly the right shapes.
3. **Tell apart** the confusable pairs: **어/오**, **으/우**, and the plain, aspirated and tense consonants.
4. **Sound out a loanword** like 아이스크림 and recognize it (ice cream).
5. **Read without romanization** next to the Hangul, even if you're tempted to peek.

Once you can do most of that, the alphabet stops being a subject and becomes a tool. Your reading will keep improving on its own as you study everything else. Sound changes between syllables, like the final consonant in **한국어** carrying over so it sounds like *han-gu-geo*, are easier to learn once the letters themselves feel familiar.

## Try this today

Learn just three letters: **ㄱ**, **ㄴ** and **ㅏ**. Write **가** and **나** five times each, saying the sound every time. Then cover the page and write them from memory. That's your first two Korean syllables, read and written in the script you'll use for the rest of the journey.
