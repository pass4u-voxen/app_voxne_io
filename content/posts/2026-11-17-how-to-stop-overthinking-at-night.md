The day goes fine. Then you turn off the light, and your mind picks up a conversation from this afternoon, an email you haven't answered, and something awkward you said three years ago. An hour later you're still awake, replaying it all and annoyed that you can't stop.

If you're searching for **how to stop overthinking at night**, the first useful thing to know is that trying harder to stop usually backfires. Telling yourself "don't think about it" keeps the topic in view. What tends to work better is giving your thoughts somewhere else to go, earlier and more gently.

## Why thoughts get louder at night

Overthinking at night isn't a sign that something is wrong with you. A few ordinary things line up at bedtime:

1. **The distractions disappear.** All day, tasks and conversations take up your attention. In a dark, quiet room, there's nothing left to compete with your worries.
2. **Unfinished business surfaces.** Things you haven't dealt with, or haven't written down, tend to come back when your mind finally has a free moment.
3. **Tiredness shrinks perspective.** When you're worn out, problems can feel bigger and more urgent than they do the next morning.
4. **Lying awake becomes its own worry.** After a few rough nights, the bed itself can start to feel like the place where you struggle, and "what if I can't sleep again" joins the list.

A helpful distinction: **problem-solving** leads to a decision or a next step. **Overthinking** goes around in a loop: the same thought, the same "what if," with no new information. At night, you're rarely in a position to act on anything, so most late thinking becomes the looping kind.

## Move the thinking earlier in the evening

Many night-time thoughts are really daytime thoughts that never got handled. Give them a time slot before bed instead.

1. **Do a 10-minute "brain dump" an hour or two before bed.** Write down everything on your mind: tasks, worries, half-finished ideas. Don't organize it yet.
2. **Turn worries into next steps.** For each item, write one small action and roughly when you'll do it ("reply to Sam: Thursday morning"). If there's nothing you can do, write "nothing to do tonight." That's an answer too.
3. **Write tomorrow's to-do list.** A short list for the next day tells your mind those tasks have a plan, so it doesn't need to keep rehearsing them.
4. **Close the notebook.** Physically closing it is a small signal that the thinking part of the day is done.

Keep a notepad and pen next to the bed as well. If a new thought shows up after lights out, jot down a few words and let it go. It's on paper, so you don't have to hold onto it.

## What to do when your mind is racing in bed

Even with a good evening routine, some nights your mind will still be busy. A few approaches people often find useful:

- **Give your mind a boring, gentle job.** One technique, sometimes called *cognitive shuffling*, works like this: pick a random word (say, "garden"), then picture one unrelated object for each letter: a glove, an apple, a rug, and so on. The images are too random to build a story around, which makes it hard for worries to hold on.
- **Slow your breathing a little.** Breathe in through your nose for a count of four and out for a slightly longer count, around six. Don't force it; just make the out-breath a bit longer than the in-breath for a few minutes.
- **Notice and label, don't argue.** When a thought comes back, name it quietly ("planning again," "that conversation again") and return to your breathing or your word game.
- **Get up if it's been a while.** If you've been lying awake for a long time (many sleep guides suggest around 20 minutes), go to another room, keep the lights low, and do something calm until you feel sleepy. Then go back to bed. This keeps the bed from becoming the place where you wrestle with your thoughts.

## Choose a calm activity for the in-between time

The activity you pick when you get up, or in the last half hour before bed, matters. A good one keeps your hands and eyes lightly busy without exciting you.

Look for something that is:

- **Slow and repetitive**, like folding laundry, knitting, a simple puzzle, or coloring.
- **Goal-light**: no scores, timers, or streaks that make you want to keep going.
- **Absorbing but not emotional**: a gentle visual task rather than the news, social media, or a tense show.

Coloring is a good example. You choose a color, fill a shape, and move to the next one, and that small amount of focus leaves little room for replaying the day. A coloring book works fine; so does a quiet coloring app with soft colors and no time pressure. If you use a screen, turn the brightness down and set a stopping point before you start. There is more on what makes an evening activity feel calm rather than stimulating in this guide to [games to play before bed](/games-to-play-before-bed/).

## Small habits that make nights easier

What you do during the day also shapes your nights:

- **Keep a regular wake-up time**, even after a poor night. It tends to make sleep more predictable over time.
- **Watch late caffeine.** Coffee in the afternoon can keep some people more alert at bedtime than they expect.
- **Leave a buffer.** Stop work messages and difficult conversations a while before bed when you can.
- **Handle stress in the daytime too.** Short resets during the day mean less is stored up for the night. The guide on [how to calm down when stressed](/how-to-calm-down-when-stressed/) has a few simple ones.

These are ordinary, everyday steps, not a cure. If overthinking keeps you awake most nights, if worries feel heavy or hard to control, or if poor sleep is affecting your days, it's worth talking to a doctor or a mental health professional. Persistent sleep problems are common, and help is available.

## One thing to try tonight

Before you get ready for bed, set a timer for ten minutes and write down everything on your mind, with one next step beside each item. Close the notebook, leave a pen by the bed, and if a thought returns, give it three words on paper instead of an hour of your night.
