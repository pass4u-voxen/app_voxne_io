You can recognize あ on a flashcard, but when someone asks you to write it, your pen stops. Or perhaps you've memorized the kana chart yet still confuse シ and ツ when you try to produce them yourself. If that sounds familiar, a **Japanese writing practice app** may be useful—but only if it makes you write rather than merely tap the correct answer.

The right tool depends on what you want to produce: individual kana, readable words, or full sentences. Before downloading anything, use the checklist below to distinguish actual handwriting practice from another recognition quiz.

## Recognition is not the same as writing

A multiple-choice question asks, “Which symbol is *ka*?” Writing asks you to retrieve the symbol, choose its strokes, and produce it without the answer in front of you. Those are different tasks.

Recognition practice is useful. It helps you connect shapes and sounds quickly. But if your goal is to handwrite Japanese, you also need **production practice**: seeing a sound or word, writing it, and checking what you produced. An app that only asks you to trace a glowing outline may feel productive without testing whether you can remember the character independently.

A good practice session uses three modes in sequence:

1. **Observe:** see a model and, where available, the correct stroke order.
2. **Trace or copy:** write with the model visible to learn the movement.
3. **Recall:** hide the model, hear or read a prompt, and write from memory.

The third step is the one beginners often skip.

## Seven features worth checking

### 1. A clear distinction between hiragana and katakana

Beginners need both syllabaries, but mixing all the characters immediately can be overwhelming. Look for a way to practice a small group of hiragana first, then a separate katakana group, before reviewing them together.

Pay particular attention to visually similar katakana such as **シ (shi)** and **ツ (tsu)**, or **ソ (so)** and **ン (n)**. Their stroke direction and placement help distinguish them. A tool should show a reliable model rather than treating any vaguely similar shape as correct.

### 2. Stroke-order demonstrations

A static character is helpful; an animated stroke sequence is more informative. Look for numbered strokes or a replayable animation. If the app checks stroke order, it should explain which stroke needs attention rather than simply marking the whole character wrong.

You can supplement an app with a [hiragana stroke-order guide](/hiragana-stroke-order/) when a character is difficult. A printed or handwritten chart works too.

### 3. A blank writing area, not just tracing

Tracing is a starting point, not the finish line. An app should let you switch to a blank area or at least hide the guide. Otherwise, you may become good at following an outline without being able to write the character from memory.

Try this quick test: after tracing き three times, hide the model and write it once. If you cannot, return to the example and try again. That's useful feedback, not failure.

### 4. Feedback that is specific enough to act on

Some tools judge handwriting by matching pixels; others focus on stroke sequence or approximate shape. Neither method is perfect. A person's handwriting naturally varies, and printed fonts do not always look exactly like handwritten kana.

Useful feedback points to something you can fix: the length of a stroke, the position of a small mark, or the order of two lines. Be cautious of a tool that repeatedly rejects legible writing without explaining why. Compare uncertain results with a trusted reference chart or a teacher.

### 5. Practice with sounds and real words

Writing symbols in isolation is only the beginning. Once you know a few kana, try writing short words from a sound prompt.

For example, hear or read *neko* and write **ねこ** (cat), or use *sushi* to recall **すし**. Keep the words simple and check both the spelling and the characters. Later, you can add katakana words such as **ホテル** (*hoteru*, hotel).

This connects handwriting to actual reading and vocabulary instead of making it a separate drawing exercise.

### 6. Review of the characters you miss

An endless list of new characters is less useful than a short list of your recurring mistakes. Look for saved mistakes, custom review sets, or a way to revisit difficult kana.

Even without an automated review feature, keep a small paper list: “シ/ツ,” “ぬ/め,” or whatever you confuse. Review those pairs on a later day. The important part is that practice responds to what you personally find difficult.

### 7. Practical controls and a fair experience

Before committing to an app, check whether writing works comfortably with your finger or stylus, whether you can erase a stroke, and whether the practice area is large enough on your screen. Check the current store listing for ads, in-app purchases, device requirements, and privacy details; these can change over time.

A beautiful interface is not enough if it interrupts every practice round or makes mistakes difficult to correct.

## Paper, stylus, or finger?

**Paper** gives you physical resistance and a familiar way to form letters. It is excellent for checking whether you can write without visual prompts.

A **stylus** can feel closer to pen writing, especially on a tablet. A **finger** is convenient for quick reviews on a phone, though small screens may make precise strokes harder. There is no need to buy special hardware just to start.

If you want to improve handwriting on paper, include some paper practice even when your main tool is digital. Skills practiced on a screen do not automatically make every pen stroke comfortable.

## A realistic 12-minute writing routine

You do not need to master the entire kana chart in one sitting. Try this repeatable session:

1. **Minutes 1–2: Review.** Read and write three characters you studied previously.
2. **Minutes 3–5: Learn.** Watch or inspect the stroke order of two new characters. Copy each a few times.
3. **Minutes 6–8: Recall.** Hide the examples and write all five characters from sound or romanized prompts.
4. **Minutes 9–10: Use words.** Write one or two simple words made from familiar kana.
5. **Minutes 11–12: Check.** Compare your writing with a reference and note one specific mistake to revisit tomorrow.

If two new characters feel like too much, learn one. Consistency and accurate checking matter more than a high daily count.

For a broader learning plan, see this [beginner's routine for learning Japanese by writing](/how-to-learn-japanese-by-writing/). It explains how to connect writing with reading and vocabulary practice.

## Questions to ask during a five-minute trial

Before you keep a Japanese writing practice app, try one character you know and one you don't. Can you see the correct stroke order? Can you write without tracing? Does the feedback explain an error? Can you revisit the same character tomorrow?

If the answers are mostly yes, the tool probably supports genuine writing practice. If it mainly rewards tapping answers or tracing indefinitely, it may still help with recognition, but it is not a complete handwriting routine.

Start today with **あ, い, and う**. Observe each one, copy it, hide the example, and write it again. The app is only a practice space; the learning happens when you try to remember and check your own writing.
