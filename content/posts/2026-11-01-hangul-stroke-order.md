You can recognize Korean letters on a screen, yet your handwriting looks uneven. Maybe **ㄱ** turns into a disconnected corner, or the vowel in **고** ends up in the wrong place. Learning **Hangul stroke order** helps with both problems: it gives each letter a consistent movement and makes syllable blocks easier to arrange.

You don't need to memorize dozens of isolated instructions. Start with a few patterns, then apply them to complete syllables.

## What does stroke order mean in Hangul?

A **stroke** is a continuous pen movement before you lift it. **Stroke order** is the sequence in which you make those movements.

Printed fonts can make a letter look like separate lines even when it is normally handwritten with a turn. For example, **ㄱ** can be written by moving right and turning down without lifting your pen. **ㄴ** moves down and then right.

Some charts count a few letters differently, particularly when a printed shape is divided into segments. Prioritize a trustworthy numbered demonstration of **direction, order and pen lifts** over guessing from a font.

## Three patterns that make most letters easier

### 1. Start at the top when strokes are stacked

When a letter has upper and lower elements, learn the upper element first. For **ㅜ**, draw the horizontal line, then the short line descending from its center. For **ㅎ**, the small mark at the top comes before the lower components.

### 2. Work from left to right

For **ㅏ**, draw the long vertical line first, then the short mark pointing right. For **ㅓ**, begin with the short mark on the left, then draw the long vertical line. The shapes are similar, but the writing sequence changes with the side of the mark.

### 3. Don't lift at every corner

A corner does not always mean a new stroke. Write **ㄱ** as a rightward line turning down and **ㄴ** as a downward line turning right. This is a key difference between handwriting and tracing a font's outline.

These patterns are starting points; check a numbered diagram for unfamiliar letters.

## Practice six consonants carefully

Begin with letters that illustrate different movements:

| Letter | Writing cue | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ㄱ** | Move right, then turn down in one stroke. | Lifting at the corner. |
| **ㄴ** | Move down, then turn right in one stroke. | Starting from the bottom. |
| **ㅁ** | Draw the left side; add the top and right side; close the bottom. | Drawing one rounded loop. |
| **ㅂ** | Draw the two upright lines, then the inner bar and bottom bar. | Starting with the bars. |
| **ㅇ** | Draw one round stroke, starting near the top and moving counterclockwise. | Drawing a disconnected circle. |
| **ㄹ** | Learn its three movements from a numbered guide. | Making one continuous zigzag. |

**Accuracy beats speed.** Write each letter slowly enough to notice your starting point and where you lift the pen. Cover the model and reproduce it once from memory.

For letters such as **ㅈ**, **ㅊ** and **ㅌ**, resources sometimes display different stroke-count conventions. Use one consistent teaching chart instead of mixing two diagrams.

## Learn vowels by the short line's position

Start by contrasting four simple vowels:

- **ㅏ (a):** long vertical first, then a short mark to the right.
- **ㅓ (eo):** short mark on the left first, then the long vertical.
- **ㅗ (o):** short upward stem first, then the horizontal line beneath it.
- **ㅜ (u):** horizontal line first, then the short downward stem.

Notice **which way the small mark points** and **which part comes first**. This helps distinguish **ㅗ** from **ㅜ**.

Add **ㅡ (eu)** and **ㅣ (i)** next. Each is one straight line: horizontal for **ㅡ**, vertical for **ㅣ**. Romanization is only a pronunciation hint; use Korean audio to learn the actual sounds.

## Write whole syllable blocks in the right order

Hangul uses **syllable blocks**. Each block contains an initial consonant, a vowel and sometimes a final consonant called **batchim** (받침). Write them **initial → vowel → final**.

The vowel determines the layout:

| Block | Components | Layout |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **나** | ㄴ + ㅏ | Vowel on the right. |
| **노** | ㄴ + ㅗ | Vowel underneath. |
| **난** | ㄴ + ㅏ + ㄴ | Final consonant at the bottom. |
| **눈** | ㄴ + ㅜ + ㄴ | Vowel below the initial; final consonant below both. |

Think of each block as fitting inside an imaginary square. Its components **share the space** rather than keeping their standalone sizes. In **난**, the upper parts must leave room for the final **ㄴ**.

## A ten-minute Hangul stroke-order drill

Use square-grid paper or a writing surface, plus a reliable reference.

1. **Minutes 0–2: Watch and trace.** Check a numbered demonstration for **ㄱ, ㄴ, ㅁ, ㅏ, ㅗ**. Trace each once.
2. **Minutes 2–4: Copy.** Write each letter twice without tracing, following the same direction.
3. **Minutes 4–6: Hide the model.** Reproduce the five letters from memory. Check movement as well as shape.
4. **Minutes 6–8: Build blocks.** Write **나, 노, 난, 눈**. Say their components first.
5. **Minutes 8–10: Correct one error.** Circle the least consistent letter and repeat it slowly three times.

A digital handwriting tool with **stroke-direction demonstrations** can work, but paper is equally useful with a good reference.

## Common mistakes to fix early

**Copying only the finished shape.** Check the movement while learning, not just the final appearance.

**Making every component full size.** A final consonant belongs inside the same syllable square, not on a separate line below it.

**Rushing through an entire chart.** Five letters with feedback are more manageable than dozens copied without checking.

**Confusing writing with pronunciation.** Handwriting helps form and recognize letters, but listening and speaking still need practice.

## Start with one square today

Choose **ㄴ**, **ㅏ** and **ㅗ**. Write the letters separately, then combine them into **나** and **노**. Notice the order of the movements and where the vowel sits.

Once those blocks look consistent, add a final consonant and try **난** and **눈**. Small, careful corrections build a better foundation than racing to fill a page.
