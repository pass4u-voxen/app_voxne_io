You've gone through the hiragana chart a dozen times, but the moment you look away, さ and き blur together again. If that's you, the problem usually isn't your memory — it's that you're only **looking**.

## Why looking isn't enough

Seeing a character over and over builds **familiarity** — that "oh yeah, I've seen this" feeling. But familiarity is not the same as being able to **produce** it on demand. When you need a character in a real sentence, there's no chart in front of you. You have to pull it out of your own head, and passive review barely trains that skill.

Hiragana makes this harder because it's a brand-new script. Unlike letters you already know, a kana you only glanced at fades fast — which is exactly why look-alikes like さ/き and シ/ツ keep tripping people up.

## Why writing makes it stick

When you write a character by hand, three things happen at once:

- **Active recall** — to write it, you have to retrieve the shape from memory. That retrieval is what strengthens the memory.
- **Motor memory** — the movement of each stroke gets stored alongside the shape, giving you an extra cue for telling similar characters apart.
- **Attention** — it's hard to zone out while writing. Those few seconds go entirely to that one character.

Writing takes more effort than looking — and that effort is precisely why it lasts.

## A 5-step write-to-remember routine

1. **Start with hiragana, in stroke order.** Don't just trace the shape — follow the set stroke order. It keeps your characters consistent and lets you write faster later.
2. **Say it out loud as you write.** Write あ while saying "a." Linking sound, shape, and movement gives memory more hooks to grab.
3. **Cover and write again.** After copying a character, **write it once more with it hidden.** This is the only real test that it actually stuck.
4. **Collect your misses.** Set aside only the characters you couldn't recall, and review those first the next day. Re-reading what you already know is wasted time.
5. **Ten minutes, same time daily.** Ten minutes every day beats one long cram session. Anchor it to something fixed, like right before bed.

## The catch: doing it every day

The method is simple — it's the **consistency** that's hard. Grabbing paper and a pen, looking up stroke order, remembering to cover and recall — doing all of that alone, every day, is where most people quit and drift back to passive flashcard apps.

Lower the setup cost until it is almost zero. Keep a stroke-order chart and a notebook on the nightstand, or use a handwriting app that already has stroke-order animations and a writing canvas, so cover-and-recall practice is one tap away instead of a small project. Whatever you pick, the rule is the same: the app or the notebook is only there to make the writing happen. The remembering still comes from your hand.

You don't need a grand start. Today, just write five hiragana — out loud, by hand. Tomorrow, cover them and see how many stuck. You'll feel the difference from "just looking" right away.
