You can read **한국** on a sign and tap the right answer in a vocabulary quiz. But when you try to write a word from memory, the blocks come out lopsided, the vowel lands in the wrong place, or you can't remember whether it was **ㅐ** or **ㅔ**. That is usually when people start looking for a **Korean handwriting practice app**.

Before you choose one, it helps to know what makes Hangul handwriting different from simply knowing the alphabet. Many language apps are built around recognition. Handwriting asks for something harder: **producing** each letter and arranging it inside a syllable block with nothing to copy from.

## Why Hangul needs its own kind of practice

Most beginners learn the 24 basic letters fairly quickly. The difficulty shows up one level higher, in the **syllable block**. Every Korean syllable is a small square built from an initial consonant, a vowel and sometimes a final consonant (**batchim**, 받침), and the vowel decides the layout:

- **Vertical vowels** such as ㅏ, ㅓ, ㅣ, ㅐ, ㅔ sit to the **right**: 가, 너, 비.
- **Horizontal vowels** such as ㅗ, ㅜ, ㅡ sit **below**: 고, 누, 크.
- **Combined vowels** such as ㅘ, ㅝ, ㅢ wrap **below and to the right**: 과, 뭐, 의.
- A **final consonant** always goes at the bottom: 각, 눈, 밥.

A tool that only drills isolated letters can leave you able to write ㄴ, ㅜ and ㄴ separately while still producing a stretched, unreadable **눈**. So the most useful question to ask of any app is not "does it teach the alphabet?" but "does it make me **build blocks from memory**?"

## Seven things to check

### 1. Block-level practice, not just letter tracing

Look for exercises where you write whole syllables and words inside a square, not only single letters on an empty canvas. Components should shrink to share the space: the ㄴ in **난** is smaller than a standalone ㄴ because it has to leave room for the final consonant underneath.

### 2. Stroke order and direction you can replay

Static charts show what a letter looks like. An animation or numbered sequence shows how it is made: **ㄱ** is one stroke moving right and turning down; **ㅏ** starts with the long vertical, then the short mark to the right. If an app's demonstrations disagree with a reliable reference, trust the reference. This [Hangul stroke order guide](/hangul-stroke-order/) covers the main patterns.

### 3. A way to hide the model

Tracing is useful the first time you meet a shape. After that, the outline does part of the work for you. A good tool lets you switch to a **blank square**, see a prompt (a sound, a romanized hint or a meaning), and write without the guide. If you can only ever trace, you are practicing following lines, not remembering letters.

### 4. Dictation: hear it, then write it

Korean spelling and pronunciation don't always match one to one, which is exactly why writing from audio is valuable. For example:

1. **같이** ("together") is pronounced roughly *gachi*.
2. **먹어요** ("I eat") sounds like *meogeoyo*, because the final ㄱ carries over into the next syllable.
3. **학교** ("school") is romanized *hakgyo*, but the second consonant sounds tenser than a plain ㄱ.

If you only ever copy text, you never discover these gaps. Hearing a word and writing it forces you to decide how it is spelled, and then shows you where your guess went wrong.

### 5. Attention to commonly confused pairs

Some letters differ by a single stroke, and a good practice tool brings them back side by side:

- **ㄱ / ㅋ**, **ㄷ / ㅌ**, **ㅈ / ㅊ**: plain versus aspirated (one extra stroke).
- **ㄱ / ㄲ**, **ㅅ / ㅆ**: plain versus tense (doubled).
- **ㅓ / ㅗ** and **ㅏ / ㅜ**: same short mark, different direction and position.
- **ㅐ / ㅔ**: pronounced almost identically by many speakers, so only spelling practice will separate them.

Whether the app does this automatically or you build your own list, the point is that review should follow **your** mistakes, not an endless stream of new material.

### 6. Feedback that tells you what to fix

Handwriting recognition is never perfect. Handwritten Hangul also varies from printed fonts: **ㅈ** is often handwritten with a shape that looks different from many typefaces, and some fonts draw the top stroke of **ㅊ** vertically while others draw it horizontally. A fair tool accepts legible variation and points to something specific, such as a stroke that starts in the wrong place or a vowel placed beside instead of below. Repeated rejection of clearly readable writing, with no explanation, wastes practice time.

### 7. Comfort on your actual device

Writing a block in a tiny square with a fingertip is frustrating. Check that the writing area is large enough, that you can undo a single stroke, and whether a stylus works. Also check the current store listing for ads, in-app purchases and privacy details, since these change over time.

## Handwriting, typing and paper: how they fit together

A practice app does not replace either of the other two:

- **Typing** on a Korean keyboard is a separate skill. You press keys for letters and the keyboard assembles the block for you, so it never tests layout.
- **Paper** is free, has no recognition errors, and is easy to review later. Square-grid paper (원고지-style or ordinary graph paper) makes block proportions visible.
- **An app** is most useful for the parts paper can't do on its own: replayable stroke demonstrations, audio prompts for dictation, and reminders of what to review.

Many learners combine them: learn a shape with a demonstration, write it a few times on paper, then use audio prompts to test recall. For a full routine built around that cycle, see [how to learn Korean by writing](/how-to-learn-korean-by-writing/).

## A five-minute test for any tool

Before committing to any app or method, try this:

1. Learn **ㅂ**, **ㅏ**, **ㄷ**, **ㅁ**, **ㅜ** and **ㄹ** with a stroke demonstration.
2. Hide every model.
3. From audio or a romanized prompt, write **바다** (*bada*, "sea") and **물** (*mul*, "water").
4. Check each block: right letters, right layout, final consonant at the bottom.

If the tool made steps 2 and 3 easy to do and gave you useful feedback on step 4, it is doing real handwriting practice. If you only ever traced, keep looking, or pair it with a notebook.

Start today with those two words. Write them once while looking, once from memory, and note which block felt least certain. That one block is tomorrow's warm-up.
