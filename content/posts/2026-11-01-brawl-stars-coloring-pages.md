Your kid can name every Brawler, argue about which one is strongest, and tell you exactly what their favorite's newest skin looks like. So when a rainy afternoon arrives and they want to color, a generic farm animal won't cut it. They want **Brawl Stars coloring pages**.

A quick search turns up plenty. Many are free, but quality varies a lot, and a few simple choices make the difference between a page that gets abandoned after five minutes and one that becomes a whole afternoon. This guide covers where these pages come from, how to pick and print good ones, and how to turn "color this" into something your child actually gets absorbed in.

A quick note first: Brawl Stars is a game made by Supercell, and nearly all the coloring pages you'll find online are **fan-made line art**, drawn by fans, not official material. That's fine for coloring at home.

## Why a favorite game character works so well on paper

Children color longer when they care about the subject. A Brawler is not just a picture to them. It's a character with a personality, a role on the team, and colors they already know by heart.

That knowledge changes the activity. A child coloring a character they love is constantly making decisions: *Do I use the real colors, or invent my own skin? Is this the version from the game, or the one I'd design?* Either way, they're thinking hard about something they're invested in, which is exactly why the attention lasts. (For the bigger picture, see [what coloring actually does for a child's mind](/coloring-games-for-kids/).)

There's also a social side. Brawl Stars is something kids talk about with friends, and a finished page of their main Brawler is a thing to show off at school, which gives the page a purpose beyond the table.

## How to choose a good coloring page

Not all line art is created equal. Before you hit print, look at the page with your child's age in mind.

1. **Line thickness.** For children around 4–6, look for thick, bold outlines and large open areas. Thin, sketchy lines and tiny details are frustrating for small hands. Older kids (8 and up) often prefer more detail because it gives them more to do.
2. **Clean, closed outlines.** Good line art has outlines that actually connect. Gaps make it harder to stay in the lines and look messy when colored.
3. **A clear pose.** Full-body poses with the character's weapon or signature item are usually the most satisfying, because kids recognize the character instantly.
4. **Enough white space.** A busy background crowds the character. A simple page lets the Brawler be the star, and kids can add their own background later.
5. **Reasonable sources.** Stick to sites that share pages freely for personal use, and avoid ones that bombard you with pop-ups or ask you to download installers just to get a PDF.

## Printing tips that make coloring easier

A few small adjustments make printed pages much nicer to color:

- **Print in black and white**, even if the page has gray shading. Gray areas confuse younger kids about where to color.
- **Use heavier paper if you have it.** Standard printer paper is fine for crayons and colored pencils, but markers tend to bleed through and wrinkle it. Cardstock or thicker paper holds up much better.
- **Scale it up.** For younger children, print the character larger, even if it means cropping the background. Bigger areas mean less frustration.
- **Print two copies.** One for the "real" colors, one for an invented version. Kids love comparing them side by side.

## Coloring ideas that go beyond "fill it in"

Once your child has a page in front of them, these ideas can stretch one sheet into a much longer, richer activity.

### Match the in-game colors

Pull up a picture of the Brawler and challenge your child to match the colors as closely as possible. It's a surprisingly good observation exercise: noticing the small details on a costume, figuring out which shade of brown or blue is closest, deciding where shadows fall.

### Design a brand-new skin

This is the favorite for many kids. Skins are alternate looks for a character, and kids think about them constantly. Ask them to invent one: a winter version, a robot version, a version in their school's colors. Then ask them to name it. You'll often get a whole story along with the drawing.

### Add a background

Plain character pages leave room for imagination. Ask where the Brawler is: a desert full of cacti for Spike, a wrestling ring for El Primo, a stage with music for Poco. Drawing a setting is good practice for kids who are just starting to draw on their own.

### Try a simple shading trick

For older kids, show them one easy technique: color the whole area in a light shade first, then go over one side with a darker version of the same color. It instantly makes the character look more three-dimensional, and it's the kind of trick they'll start using on everything.

## Paper, screen, or both

Printed pages are great for the tactile side: real pencils, real pressure, and a page they can hold and stick on the fridge. A coloring app with game-style characters offers different advantages: unlimited undo, no printer, perfect fills for younger kids, and no mess on the kitchen table.

Whichever you use, the same rules apply: keep it pressure-free, let them choose the colors, and ask about their choices instead of correcting them. If they want a pink Brawler, that's a design decision, not a mistake.

## Make a little gallery

Kids put more care into a picture when they know someone will see it. Pick a spot on the wall or fridge for finished Brawlers, or keep a folder of their best pages. Over a few weeks, it becomes a collection they're proud of, and looking back at early pages shows them how much more control they've gained.

**Try it today:** ask your child which Brawler they'd most like to color, print one page in black and white, and give them a second copy to invent a skin of their own. Then ask them to tell you about it. You'll learn more about the game than you ever expected.
