You searched for a **hiragana chart**, so you probably want two things: the full set of characters on one screen, and some idea of what to do with it. A chart is a reference, not a lesson. Stared at, it blurs into 46 squiggles. Read the right way, it's a tidy system that tells you most of what you need to know about Japanese sounds.

Below is the complete chart, then how to read it, the handful of irregular spots, the extra marks that turn 46 characters into over a hundred sounds, and a sensible order for learning it.

## The basic hiragana chart

Hiragana is arranged as a grid called the **gojūon** ("fifty sounds"). Each row is a consonant, each column a vowel. Read across in the order **a, i, u, e, o**, which is the Japanese vowel order, not the English one.

| | a | i | u | e | o |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **–** | あ a | い i | う u | え e | お o |
| **k** | か ka | き ki | く ku | け ke | こ ko |
| **s** | さ sa | し **shi** | す su | せ se | そ so |
| **t** | た ta | ち **chi** | つ **tsu** | て te | と to |
| **n** | な na | に ni | ぬ nu | ね ne | の no |
| **h** | は ha | ひ hi | ふ **fu** | へ he | ほ ho |
| **m** | ま ma | み mi | む mu | め me | も mo |
| **y** | や ya | | ゆ yu | | よ yo |
| **r** | ら ra | り ri | る ru | れ re | ろ ro |
| **w** | わ wa | | | | を wo (o) |
| | ん n | | | | |

Count them and you get 46. The gaps are real: modern Japanese has no *yi*, *ye*, *wi*, *wu* or *we* sounds in everyday hiragana, so those cells stay empty.

## How to read the grid

Once you see the pattern, most of the chart reads itself. Take a consonant, add a vowel: **k + a = ka**, **m + o = mo**. You aren't memorizing 46 sounds. You're memorizing 5 vowels, 9 consonant rows and *ん*, then combining them.

Japanese vowels are short and steady: *a* as in "father," *i* as in "machine," *u* like the "oo" in "food" but with relaxed lips, *e* as in "get," *o* as in "go" without the glide at the end. Every character in a row keeps the same vowel sound, so if you can say the top row, you can pronounce the rest.

### The irregular spots

A few cells don't follow the simple consonant + vowel spelling. These are the ones worth circling on any chart you print:

1. **し = shi**, not *si*.
2. **ち = chi** and **つ = tsu**, not *ti* and *tu*.
3. **ふ = fu**, a soft sound between *f* and *h*, made by blowing lightly through barely-parted lips.
4. **を = wo**, usually pronounced simply *o*. It's used almost only as a grammar particle that marks the object of a verb.
5. **ん = n**, the only character that is a consonant on its own. It almost never starts a word.
6. **The R row** is a light tap of the tongue, somewhere between English *r*, *l* and *d*. Don't roll it and don't make it a hard English *r*.

Two everyday quirks also come from grammar rather than the chart: when **は** is the topic particle it's read *wa*, and when **へ** is the direction particle it's read *e*. Everywhere else they're *ha* and *he*.

## Beyond the 46: dakuten, handakuten and combinations

The basic chart is only the foundation. Three small additions give you the rest of the sounds in standard Japanese, and none of them requires a new character shape.

**Dakuten (゛)**, two short strokes at the upper right, voice the consonant:

| | a | i | u | e | o |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **k → g** | が ga | ぎ gi | ぐ gu | げ ge | ご go |
| **s → z** | ざ za | じ ji | ず zu | ぜ ze | ぞ zo |
| **t → d** | だ da | ぢ ji | づ zu | で de | ど do |
| **h → b** | ば ba | び bi | ぶ bu | べ be | ぼ bo |

ぢ and づ sound the same as じ and ず and appear only in a small number of words, so beginners can treat じ and ず as the default spellings.

**Handakuten (゜)**, a small circle, turns the H row into P: **ぱ pa, ぴ pi, ぷ pu, ぺ pe, ぽ po**. As in えんぴつ (*enpitsu*, pencil).

**Combinations (yōon)** pair an *-i* character with a small ゃ, ゅ or ょ to make one syllable: きゃ *kya*, しゅ *shu*, ちょ *cho*, じゃ *ja*, りょ *ryo*. The small character is written at roughly half size. Compare **きよ** (*kiyo*, two syllables) with **きょ** (*kyo*, one).

**Small っ** doesn't make a sound of its own. It doubles the consonant that follows, creating a tiny pause: きて (*kite*, "come") versus きって (*kitte*, "stamp").

**Long vowels** are usually written by adding a vowel character: おかあさん (*okaasan*, mother), ありがとう (*arigatō*, thank you). A long *o* is most often spelled with う rather than お, as in that same ありがとう.

## The order to learn the chart

The traditional order is simply the rows from top to bottom: **a, ka, sa, ta, na, ha, ma, ya, ra, wa, n**. Japanese speakers learn it as a chant, *a-ka-sa-ta-na, ha-ma-ya-ra-wa*, and it's also the order Japanese dictionaries use, so it's worth knowing for that alone.

A workable plan:

1. **One row at a time.** Five characters is a manageable chunk. Don't move on until you can write the row from memory, not just recognize it.
2. **Learn the irregular cells as you reach them.** Each row has at most two surprises.
3. **Add dakuten and handakuten after the base chart.** They're shape changes to characters you already know, so they go quickly.
4. **Save combinations and small っ for last.** They're spelling rules, and they make more sense once you're reading real words.
5. **Read real words early.** ねこ (*neko*, cat), いぬ (*inu*, dog), さかな (*sakana*, fish), ごはん (*gohan*, rice or a meal). A word you can read is a much better reward than a filled-in row.

## How to use a chart without leaning on it

The chart's job is to check your answers, not to give them to you. Learners who keep it open while reading often find, weeks later, that they still glance at it for every other character.

- **Print a blank grid.** Fill it in from memory, then check against the full chart. The empty cells you hesitate over are your study list for tomorrow.
- **Write, don't just look.** Copying a character while saying its sound links the shape, the movement and the sound together. A notebook with large squares is enough; a handwriting app with stroke-order animations does the same job on a phone.
- **Cover a column, not a row.** Once you know the rows, quiz yourself down a vowel column (*ka, sa, ta, na…*). It breaks the habit of reciting the chant to find one character.
- **Put the chart away for a day.** If you can fill a blank grid without it, you've learned the chart. If you can't, you know which rows need another pass.

A small thing to do today: draw a five-by-eleven grid on a sheet of paper, fill in every character you already know without looking, and then use the chart above to fill in the rest in a different color. That second color is your next week of practice.
