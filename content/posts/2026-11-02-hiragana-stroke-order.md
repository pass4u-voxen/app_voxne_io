You can draw a passable あ. Then you check a stroke-order chart and see that you've been starting it in the wrong place. Is that a real problem, or just pedantry?

It matters more than it seems, and learning it is easier than it looks. **Hiragana stroke order** isn't 46 separate things to memorize. A handful of principles cover almost every character, and only a small group of exceptions need special attention.

## Why stroke order matters (even if the result looks the same)

Learners are told to follow stroke order but rarely told why. There are three practical reasons.

1. **Your characters come out consistent.** The same sequence of movements gives you the same shape every time. Proportions that drift when you improvise, like the loop in あ or the gap in い, settle into place.
2. **Look-alikes become easier to separate.** ぬ and め, ね, れ and わ, は and ほ: on the page they differ by a detail. In your hand they are different motions. Once each character has its own sequence, the motion helps you remember which is which.
3. **Fast handwriting stays readable.** When you write quickly, strokes start to connect. Japanese handwriting connects them in the order they're written, so a character written in the standard order still looks right when it gets loose. One written in a private order turns into a scribble.

A fixed sequence also turns each character into a short, repeatable movement, which is easier to recall than an image you've only looked at. That's the core idea behind [learning Japanese by writing](/how-to-learn-japanese-by-writing/): the shape you produce yourself sticks better than the shape you merely recognize.

## The basic rules

These come from the same tradition as kanji stroke order.

1. **Top to bottom.** Upper parts are written before lower parts. In さ, the horizontal line comes first and the bottom curve last.
2. **Left to right.** In い, the longer left stroke comes before the shorter right one. In に, the vertical on the left comes before the two short horizontals.
3. **Horizontal before vertical when they cross.** In あ, お, す, ま and ほ, the horizontal stroke is written before the vertical one that crosses it.
4. **Small dots and finishing marks usually come last.** The dot in お, か and む, and the two side dots in ふ, are added after the main body.
5. **Each stroke has a specific ending.** Strokes end in one of three ways: a **stop** (*tome*), a small **flick** (*hane*), or a tapering **sweep** (*harai*). The left stroke of い ends with a little flick up toward the right stroke. The end of the last stroke in し sweeps up and away. Copy endings from a model rather than ending every stroke the same way.

## The characters beginners get wrong most often

Here are the ones to check against a stroke-order chart first. Stroke counts follow the standard school forms.

1. **あ (a), 3 strokes.** Horizontal, then the vertical that curves slightly, then the big looping stroke. Many learners start with the loop.
2. **か (ka), 3 strokes.** First the stroke that goes across and then hooks down, then the separate slanted stroke through the middle, then the dot on the right, last.
3. **き (ki), 4 strokes, and さ (sa), 3 strokes.** The horizontals come first, then the diagonal, then the bottom curve. Printed fonts often show a gap before the bottom curve. In handwriting it's common to connect them, and both are correct.
4. **せ (se), 3 strokes.** The long horizontal first, then the short vertical on the right, then the left vertical that curves along the bottom.
5. **た (ta), 4 strokes, and な (na), 4 strokes.** Both start with the horizontal and the diagonal, the same way. た finishes with a small こ shape on the right. な finishes with a dot, then a vertical with a loop at the bottom.
6. **も (mo), 3 strokes.** This is the famous exception. The curved vertical stroke comes **first**, and the two horizontal strokes cross it afterward. It breaks rule 3, which is exactly why people get it wrong.
7. **ね (ne), れ (re), わ (wa), 2 strokes each.** All three begin with the same vertical stroke. The second stroke is what differs: it ends in a loop for ね, a flick outward for れ, and a rounded curve for わ.
8. **ぬ (nu) and め (me), 2 strokes each.** The same short diagonal first, then the long crossing stroke. ぬ ends with a small loop; め doesn't. Writing them side by side makes the difference stick.
9. **ふ (fu), 4 strokes.** Top stroke, then the middle curve, then the left dot, then the right dot. It's one of the few hiragana with four separate pieces.
10. **や (ya), 3 strokes.** The large curved stroke, then the small dot, then the long vertical that crosses the curve.

Single-stroke characters like く, し, つ, て, の, へ, る, ろ and ん have no order to get wrong. Just pay attention to direction and how each stroke ends.

## How to practice stroke order without it becoming a chore

Stroke order sticks through repetition with feedback, not staring at a chart:

1. **Watch it once.** Use an animated stroke-order chart or a video. A static chart with tiny numbered arrows is easy to misread. Seeing the stroke actually move is clearer.
2. **Write it slowly three times, saying the sound.** Count the strokes out loud if that helps: "a: one, two, three."
3. **Cover the model and write it from memory.** This is the step that actually tests you. If you hesitate over which stroke comes next, you haven't learned it yet.
4. **Check against the model.** Compare the order and the endings, not just the shape.
5. **Group the look-alikes.** Practice ね, れ, わ together, ぬ and め together, は and ほ together. Contrast teaches faster than isolation.

Square-grid practice sheets help keep proportions even. If you prefer a screen, a handwriting app that animates each stroke and lets you write on top is a convenient way to get the "watch it, then write it" loop in a few taps. Either way, the work is in your hand, not in the tool.

Ten minutes a day on five or six characters is plenty. With steady practice the standard order starts to feel automatic, and you'll mostly need the chart for the exceptions above.

## A small step for today

Pick three characters from the list above, say あ, か and も. Write each one five times following the correct order, then cover the chart and write them once more from memory. If the sequence comes out right without thinking, you're ready for the next three tomorrow.
