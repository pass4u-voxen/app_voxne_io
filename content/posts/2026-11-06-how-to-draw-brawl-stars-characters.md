Your child wants to draw their favorite Brawler. They start with the eyes, get the head too small, the body doesn't fit on the page, and two minutes later the paper is crumpled and they're announcing that they "can't draw."

The good news: **drawing Brawl Stars characters** is easier than drawing most cartoon characters, and the reason is built into how they're designed. Once you see it, you can teach it to a child in an afternoon.

A quick note: Brawl Stars is a game by Supercell. Drawing its characters at home for fun is fan art, the same as drawing any character a child loves.

## Why kids get stuck (and why it isn't talent)

Most children draw the way they look at a face: details first. They start with an eye, then another eye, then a mouth, and only then try to fit a head around it. The proportions go wrong almost immediately, and because the details are already there, the drawing feels impossible to fix.

Experienced artists do the opposite. They start with **big, simple shapes**, check that the shapes look right, and only add details at the end. It's not a talent difference. It's an order-of-operations difference, and it's completely learnable.

Characters from games like Brawl Stars are ideal for practicing this, because they're designed to be read instantly on a small phone screen. That means:

- **Chunky proportions.** Big heads, short bodies, stubby limbs. Many characters are only two or three "heads" tall.
- **Strong silhouettes.** Each character is recognizable from its outline alone: a hat, a hairstyle, a weapon, a costume shape.
- **Few, bold details.** Small features get dropped because they wouldn't show up mid-match.

All of that makes them friendlier to draw than realistic figures. You're mostly stacking simple shapes and then adding the two or three things that make the character *that* character.

## The shape-first method, step by step

Use a regular pencil and press lightly for the first four steps. Light lines are easy to erase, which takes a lot of pressure off.

1. **Pick one character and one reference picture.** A clear, front-facing image works best. Fewer accessories means an easier first drawing.
2. **Draw the head as a simple shape.** Usually a circle or a rounded square. Make it big; on these characters, the head is often as large as the whole body. Place it in the upper part of the page so there's room below.
3. **Add the body as a second shape.** A rounded rectangle, a bean, or a pear shape, joined right under the head. Compare against the reference: is the body about as tall as the head? Shorter? Adjust now, while it's just shapes.
4. **Add arms and legs as tubes or ovals.** Short and thick. Hands can start as mittens or circles.
5. **Find the "signature" features.** Ask your child: *what would tell a friend who this is, even from far away?* It might be a hat, a hairstyle, a mask, a flower, or the thing they hold. Sketch just those big shapes first. A character who is a cactus, for example, is mostly an upright oval with arms and a few spikes.
6. **Place the face.** Draw a faint horizontal line across the middle of the head; eyes usually sit on or near it. Simple dots or ovals are enough. Big expressive eyes and a clear mouth do most of the work.
7. **Go over the lines you want to keep.** Press harder, or use a marker or fine pen once the pencil is right. Then erase the leftover construction lines.
8. **Color last.** Big flat areas of color first, then any shading. This is where the character really comes alive.

If a step goes wrong, go back one step rather than starting over. A head that's too small is a five-second fix at step 3 and a crumpled page at step 7.

## Tips that make a real difference

**Draw small first.** A quick sketch the size of a matchbox lets a child test the proportions in under a minute. Once it looks right, they can draw the big version with confidence.

**Squint at the reference.** Squinting blurs the details and leaves only the big shapes.

**Trace once, then draw.** Tracing over a printed picture isn't cheating as a learning step. It lets a child feel how the shapes connect. The next drawing is done without tracing, using what they noticed.

**Simplify on purpose.** Leave out the pattern on a jacket or the extra straps. If the silhouette and the signature feature are right, the character will be recognizable.

**Expect the first one to be wobbly.** The third drawing of the same character is usually much better than the first. Drawing one favorite character several times teaches more than drawing ten different ones once.

## Helping a child stick with it

The biggest obstacle for most kids isn't skill. It's the moment the drawing doesn't look like the picture in their head. A few things help:

- **Praise the decisions, not the result.** "You got his hat shape exactly right" is more useful than "it's great," because it tells them what worked.
- **Draw alongside them.** Your drawing doesn't need to be good. A parent cheerfully making a lopsided version is the best proof that wobbly drawings are allowed.
- **Keep the sessions short.** One character, start to finish, is a complete session. Stop while it's still fun.
- **Keep a folder.** Drawings from a month ago are the clearest evidence a child has that they're improving.

For some kids, especially younger ones, coloring is the easier entry point. Filling in clean line art of a favorite character lets them focus on color and detail without the frustration of proportions, and it quietly teaches them how the character is built. Many children go back and forth between the two: color a few pages, then try drawing the same character themselves. ## Try it today

Ask your child which Brawler they'd most like to draw, find one clear picture, and do steps 1 to 4 together: just the head, body, arms and legs, in light pencil. Ten minutes, nothing else. Once the shapes look right, they'll want to finish it on their own.
