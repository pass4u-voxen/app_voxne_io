You studied last week. You were sure you knew those words. Today you open your notes and half of them look like strangers, and the kana you "finished" a month ago now takes a second too long to read. If you're asking **why can't I remember Japanese**, you're far from alone, and nothing is wrong with your brain.

What's usually going on is that "I can't remember Japanese" is really several different problems that feel the same. Forgetting a kana shape, forgetting a word, and freezing when you need a word in conversation have different causes, so they need different fixes. Doing more of the same study rarely helps. Working out *which* kind of forgetting you have usually does.

## First, the part that's normal

Forgetting isn't a sign of failure. It's what memory does by default. New material fades fastest in the first day or two, and each time you bring it back from memory after some of it has faded, it tends to last a little longer. That's why one big session feels great and then seems to vanish, while short, spaced sessions feel harder but stick better.

Japanese adds some difficulties of its own for English speakers: three scripts, very few familiar-looking words outside of loanwords, and kanji that can have more than one reading. It's reasonable for it to take longer than you hoped. The goal is to stop losing ground you've already covered.

## The five kinds of forgetting

### 1. You forget the kana

**Symptom:** You learned hiragana, but some characters blur together or take a long pause to read, especially look-alikes such as ぬ (*nu*) and め (*me*), or ね (*ne*), れ (*re*) and わ (*wa*).

**Likely cause:** You learned to *recognize* the characters, but you rarely had to produce them, and you stopped reading kana every day once you felt done.

**Fix:** Spend two minutes a day writing the troublesome characters from their sound alone, then checking. Read real kana every day, even just a few lines. If the whole set feels shaky, go back to [a recall-first hiragana plan](/how-to-memorize-hiragana/) for a week instead of pushing ahead.

### 2. You forget words you "learned"

**Symptom:** A word looks familiar on the card, but the next day you can't bring it to mind from its English meaning.

**Likely cause:** Rereading. Looking at a word and its translation side by side makes it *feel* known without you ever practicing pulling it out of memory. The familiarity is real, but it isn't the same as being able to recall the word.

**Fix:** Always hide the answer before you try. Test both directions: Japanese to meaning, and meaning to Japanese. Add fewer words: five words you can actually recall are worth more than twenty you only recognize. There's a full routine for this in [how to memorize Japanese words](/best-way-to-memorize-japanese-words/).

### 3. You read romaji instead of Japanese

**Symptom:** You know *konnichiwa*, but you hesitate over こんにちは, and you're not sure why it ends in は rather than わ.

**Likely cause:** Learning through romaji means you've memorized an English-letter spelling, not the Japanese. Every time you meet the real script, it's a different and weaker memory. (The は here is the topic particle, which is pronounced *wa*.)

**Fix:** Drop romaji as soon as you can read hiragana, even slowly. Slow reading in kana builds the right memory, and fast reading in romaji builds the wrong one.

### 4. You recognize words in text but not in speech

**Symptom:** You understand a sentence when you read it, but the same sentence spoken at natural speed sounds like one long word.

**Likely cause:** Your memory of the word is visual. Spoken Japanese also contracts things: ではない (*dewa nai*) often becomes じゃない (*ja nai*) in casual speech, and vowels in words like です (*desu*) are often barely voiced.

**Fix:** Learn each new word with audio from a reliable source, and say it aloud yourself. Re-listen to short clips you've already read, so your ear can connect the sound to something you understand.

### 5. You know the word but freeze when you need it

**Symptom:** You'd get it right on a quiz, but in a conversation or when writing a message, nothing comes out.

**Likely cause:** You've only practiced the word in isolation. Using a word means retrieving it quickly *and* fitting it into a sentence, which is a separate skill.

**Fix:** For every word, practice at least one short sentence you might actually say. 駅に行きます (*eki ni ikimasu*, "I'm going to the station") is more usable than 駅 = station on its own. Write two or three of these sentences from memory each day.

## Habits that quietly cause forgetting

Even with the right methods, a few patterns make Japanese slip away:

- **Long gaps.** Three hours on Sunday and nothing until next Sunday loses more than twenty minutes a day. Frequency matters more than session length.
- **Always adding, never reviewing.** If every session starts with new material, older material has nowhere to come back.
- **Treating kanji readings as one thing.** 日 is read *nichi* in 日曜日 (*nichiyōbi*), *hi* in 日 on its own, and *ka* in 二日 (*futsuka*). Learn readings inside words, not as a list attached to the character.
- **Copying instead of recalling.** Writing a word ten times while looking at it trains your hand to copy. Writing it once from memory, then checking, tests what you actually know.

## What helps it stick

The common thread in the fixes is simple: **make your brain produce the answer, then check it, then do it again a bit later.** Handwriting is a natural way to do this for kana and short words, because a blank page gives you no hints and shows you exactly which stroke or syllable you lost. A notebook works fine. So does a handwriting app with stroke-order animations, if you prefer to practice on a phone.

A sustainable daily shape looks like this:

1. **Five minutes:** review yesterday's items from memory before looking at anything.
2. **Five minutes:** learn a small number of new items, each with audio and an example sentence.
3. **Three minutes:** write the new items from their meaning or sound, then check.
4. **Two minutes:** read or listen to a few lines of real Japanese.

Fifteen minutes done most days usually beats an occasional long session.

## Try this today

Take the last ten words or kana you studied. Cover the Japanese and write each one from its meaning or sound. Whatever you miss is your review list for tomorrow. It's short, specific, and much more useful than rereading everything you've ever learned.
