You can recognize a few Korean letters on a chart, but when you try to write a word without looking, the shapes disappear. Or you can read **가** on your phone yet have no idea how to put it on paper. If that sounds familiar, here's **how to learn Korean by writing**—without copying entire pages or memorizing a huge chart first.

The key is to move between three actions: **look, write, and recall**. Copying gives you a starting point; recalling the shape without the example tells you what you actually remember.

## First, understand what you're writing

Hangul is the Korean writing system. Its letters are arranged into **syllable blocks**, rather than written in one long row like the letters in an English word.

Start with a few basic pieces:

- **ㄱ** is a consonant often romanized as *g* or *k*, depending on context.
- **ㄴ** is a consonant usually romanized as *n*.
- **ㅏ** is a vowel romanized as *a*.
- **ㅗ** is a vowel romanized as *o*.
- **ㅇ** is silent at the start of a syllable, but represents an *ng* sound when it appears at the end.

Now combine them into blocks:

| Pieces | Block | What to notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ㄱ + ㅏ | **가** | The vowel ㅏ sits to the right of ㄱ. |
| ㄴ + ㅏ | **나** | The same right-side vowel works with ㄴ. |
| ㅇ + ㅏ | **아** | The initial ㅇ has no sound here. |
| ㄱ + ㅗ | **고** | The vowel ㅗ sits underneath ㄱ. |
| ㅎ + ㅏ + ㄴ | **한** | The final ㄴ goes at the bottom of the block. |

This layout matters more than beautiful handwriting on day one. When you understand the blocks, a new word becomes a small construction puzzle instead of an unfamiliar drawing.

**Pronunciation note:** romanization is only a rough guide. Listen to native audio as you learn; English letters cannot represent every Korean sound precisely.

## Step 1: Write five shapes, not the whole alphabet

For your first session, choose **ㄱ, ㄴ, ㅇ, ㅏ, and ㅗ**. Keep the list short enough that you can check every attempt.

1. Look at one letter and a reliable stroke-order demonstration.
2. Write it slowly three times, following that order.
3. Hide the example and write it once from memory.
4. Reveal the example and compare: Did you preserve the shape and stroke direction?
5. Circle the letters you missed; those are tomorrow's starting point.

Pay attention to the difference between **ㅏ**, which has a vertical main line, and **ㅗ**, which has a horizontal main line. That difference also changes how the syllable block is arranged.

Don't speed up just to fill a page. Five accurate attempts with a memory check give you clearer feedback than fifty copies you never test.

## Step 2: Turn letters into readable blocks

Once the five shapes feel familiar, write these four blocks in order:

**가 → 나 → 아 → 고**

For each one, say what it contains before writing it. For example: “ㄱ plus ㅏ makes 가.” Then write the whole block without referring to the example.

Try a second round in reverse. Look at **고**, cover it, and reconstruct the pieces. This tests whether you know the components rather than merely remembering the overall silhouette.

A useful check is to ask two questions after each block: **Which consonant begins it? Where does the vowel go?** The answer determines the layout.

## Step 3: Use real words early

You don't have to wait until you've mastered every consonant to start writing words. Try these beginner-friendly examples once you have learned their component letters:

- **나** — “I” or “me” in informal contexts.
- **아이** — “child” (아 + 이).
- **가구** — “furniture” (가 + 구).
- **한국** — “Korea” (한 + 국).

For the last three, look up any new letters and their correct stroke order before copying. You are **not** expected to know all of them in your first session.

Write a word while looking, then cover it and write it again. Afterward, check each block separately. If **한국** is wrong, was the problem the first block, the second block, or the placement of a final consonant? A specific correction is easier to act on than “I forgot the word.”

## A realistic 15-minute practice plan

Here's a short routine you can repeat without turning Korean study into another long task:

1. **Minutes 0–3 — Review.** Write yesterday's two hardest letters from memory before opening a chart.
2. **Minutes 3–7 — Learn.** Practice two new letters with their proper stroke order.
3. **Minutes 7–11 — Combine.** Build three syllable blocks using familiar consonants and vowels.
4. **Minutes 11–14 — Recall.** Cover your examples and rewrite the blocks and one short word.
5. **Minute 14–15 — Record.** Note one shape or block to revisit tomorrow.

On a busy day, do only the recall step. A short honest check of what you remember is more useful than pretending you've studied by scrolling through a chart.

## The difference between tracing and remembering

Tracing is useful when a shape is brand new. It shows you where the strokes go. But if you **only** trace, the guide is doing part of the work for you.

Use a three-pass sequence instead:

- **Trace once** to learn the movement.
- **Copy once** while looking at the finished letter.
- **Recall once** with the reference hidden.

If recall fails, return to the example and try again. That is not a setback; it is the information you needed from the exercise.

You can do this on paper, on a tablet, or on a phone that supports handwriting. Choose the format that makes it easiest to practice consistently.
