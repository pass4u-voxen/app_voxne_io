A friend posts a photo from a narrow street in Lisbon. You look up from your desk and feel it instantly: the pull to be *anywhere* but here. Then reality answers back. No vacation days left, a budget that says no, people who depend on you. So you close the photo and go back to work, and the itch quietly gets worse.

If you're trying to figure out **how to deal with wanderlust** when a real trip isn't on the table, the good news is that the longing is manageable. The bad news is that the two most common responses, waiting it out and scrolling travel feeds, both make it stronger.

## Why "just wait until your next trip" doesn't work

Wanderlust feels like it's about a destination, but it rarely is. What most people miss is a handful of **feelings** that travel reliably delivers:

- **Novelty.** Unfamiliar streets, food, sounds. Your attention wakes up because nothing is on autopilot.
- **Absorption.** On a good trip, you're fully in the place. There's no half-attention, no tab-switching.
- **Slowness.** A café with no agenda. A walk with no destination.
- **Anticipation.** The weeks before a trip are often as enjoyable as the trip itself.

None of these require a passport. When you postpone all of them until "someday," you go months without any of them, and the longing has nowhere to go. Meanwhile, travel feeds offer the sights without the feelings. You see Santorini, but you're still slumped on the sofa, comparing your Tuesday to someone's highlight reel. That's why thirty minutes of travel scrolling usually ends with you feeling further from the world, not closer.

The way out is to feed the feelings directly, in small doses, starting this week.

## 1. Name what you're actually missing

Before you do anything, get specific. Finish this sentence honestly: *"When I imagine being away, the part I want most is…"*

- *…seeing something beautiful* → you're missing **beauty and novelty**.
- *…nobody needing anything from me* → you're missing **slowness and anonymity**.
- *…figuring out a new place* → you're missing **discovery**.
- *…the countdown* → you're missing **anticipation**.

Each answer points to a different fix. Someone who misses slowness doesn't need a packed "virtual travel" itinerary. They need one unhurried hour.

## 2. Give your next trip a real date, even a far one

A trip that exists only as "someday" can't be looked forward to. One that exists as "spring 2028, Kyoto, roughly this budget" can, and anticipation is a large part of what makes travel feel good.

1. Pick **one** destination you keep coming back to. Not a list. One.
2. Choose a season and a rough year. It can be two or three years out.
3. Start a single note with three sections: *things I want to see*, *rough costs*, *small savings*.
4. Add to it whenever wanderlust hits. This turns an itch into a plan.

The note does something scrolling can't: every time you open it, the trip gets slightly more real.

## 3. Be a tourist in your own city for one afternoon

Novelty and discovery are available within a bus ride of where you live. You've just stopped looking.

1. Pick a neighborhood you've **never** walked through on foot.
2. Go without a plan and, if you can, without checking the map.
3. Do the things you'd do abroad: order something you can't pronounce, step into a shop you'd normally pass, sit somewhere and watch people.
4. Take exactly one photo, the way you would on a trip. Then put the phone away.

Two hours of this gives you more of the *feeling* of travel than two weeks of saved reels.

## 4. Build a 20-minute "elsewhere" ritual at home

This is the one to repeat. Choose one city and spend twenty minutes there, using as many senses as you can:

- **Sound:** a playlist of music from that country, not a generic "chill" mix.
- **Taste:** one drink or snack that belongs to the place. Matcha for Kyoto, a strong small coffee for Istanbul.
- **Hands:** something slow to do while you're "there": a book set in the city, a film, a sketch, or coloring a scene of its streets.

The hands part matters most. Watching a city is passive; making something about it is absorbing, which is exactly the feeling travel gives you and scrolling doesn't.

## 5. Keep the trip going with a small record

Travelers keep journals and collect stamps for a reason: looking back at where you've "been" extends the pleasure. Do the same with your at-home trips. A list of neighborhoods explored, cities "visited" from the sofa, a line or two about each. It's a small thing, and it quietly turns wanderlust from an ache into a collection.

## Start tonight with one city

Don't overhaul your evenings. Pick the single city you can't stop thinking about, put on music from there, make the drink, and spend twenty minutes making something about it. Then write one line in your record.

Wanderlust doesn't go away by being ignored. It settles down when you give it somewhere to go, a little at a time, until the real trip comes around.
