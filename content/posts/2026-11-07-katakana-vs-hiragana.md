You've just started learning Japanese and found out there are two alphabets. Both have 46 basic characters, both cover exactly the same sounds, and some of the characters look almost identical. So the natural question is why both exist, and which one you're supposed to be reading at any given moment.

The short answer: **hiragana and katakana are not two alphabets for two sets of sounds. They're two sets of letters for the same sounds, given different jobs.** Once you see the jobs, the "why" stops being confusing, and telling them apart gets a lot easier.

## Same sounds, different roles

Both scripts are **kana**: syllabaries in which each character stands for a syllable, like *ka*, *mi* or *so*. Both grew out of Chinese characters that were once borrowed just for their sound. Hiragana came from flowing, cursive versions of whole characters, which is why it looks soft and rounded. Katakana came from small pieces of characters, written quickly as reading notes beside texts, which is why it looks short and angular.

The sound inventory is shared. か and カ are both *ka*. す and ス are both *su*. Learning katakana after hiragana doesn't mean learning new sounds. You're learning a second set of shapes for a chart you already know.

What differs is how each one is used.

**Hiragana** is the everyday, grammatical script. It's used for:

1. **Grammar**: particles such as は (*wa*), を (*o*) and に (*ni*), and the endings that attach to verbs and adjectives (the べる in 食べる, *taberu*, "to eat").
2. **Native words** with no kanji, or whose kanji is rare or hard.
3. **Reading aids**: the small furigana printed above kanji to show pronunciation.
4. **Children's books and beginner material**, written mostly or entirely in hiragana.

**Katakana** is the "marked" script. It's used for:

1. **Loanwords** from other languages: コーヒー (*kōhī*, coffee), テレビ (*terebi*, TV), ホテル (*hoteru*, hotel).
2. **Foreign names and places**: your own name, if it isn't Japanese, will almost always be written in katakana.
3. **Sound effects and onomatopoeia**, especially in manga.
4. **Emphasis**, a bit like italics in English, and some technical uses such as the names of plants and animals in scientific writing.

A typical Japanese sentence mixes kanji for most content words, hiragana for the grammar holding them together, and katakana for anything borrowed or specially marked. Think of katakana as a highlighter that signals "this word came from outside" or "notice this."

## How to tell them apart at a glance

**Look at the strokes.** Hiragana is curvy: loops, hooks and sweeping lines, as in あ, の, ゆ and る. Katakana is built from short, mostly straight strokes and sharp corners, as in ア, ノ, ユ and ル. If a character looks as though it was drawn with a ruler, it's probably katakana.

**Look for the long dash.** Katakana marks a long vowel with a line, ー (horizontal in left-to-right text, vertical in vertical text). コーヒー has two. Hiragana usually writes a long vowel by adding another vowel letter instead: おかあさん (*okāsan*, mother). A ー in the middle of a word is a strong sign you're reading katakana.

**Look for unusual combinations.** To write sounds Japanese doesn't traditionally have, katakana pairs a character with a small vowel: ファ (*fa*), ティ (*ti*), ウィ (*wi*). These show up almost only in katakana loanwords.

**Expect a few near-twins.** Because some characters in each set grew from the same original, a handful look alike across scripts: へ and ヘ (*he*) are practically identical, and か/カ, き/キ, せ/セ, も/モ, や/ヤ and り/リ are close cousins. That's actually useful. Those pairs are the easiest katakana to learn once you know the hiragana.

## The katakana pairs that trip everyone up

Most beginners don't confuse hiragana with katakana for long. The real trouble is inside katakana itself, with two famous pairs:

1. **シ (*shi*) vs ツ (*tsu*).** Both are two short strokes plus a longer one. In シ, the two short strokes sit stacked on the left, and the long stroke **sweeps upward** from bottom-left to top-right. In ツ, the two short strokes sit side by side along the top, and the long stroke **comes down** from the top-right toward the bottom-left.
2. **ン (*n*) vs ソ (*so*).** Each is one short stroke plus one long one. In ン, the long stroke **rises** from the bottom-left, like シ. In ソ, it **falls** from the top, like ツ.

Here's the useful part: in print, the difference is mostly the *angle*, which is easy to misread. In handwriting, it's the *direction your pen travels*, which is very hard to forget once you've done it. Rising stroke: シ and ン. Falling stroke: ツ and ソ. This is a good example of why stroke order matters. The same rules that keep your [hiragana stroke order](/hiragana-stroke-order/) tidy are what keep these four distinct.

## Which to learn first, and how to practice both

Learn **hiragana first.** You'll need it in every sentence, it's what beginner textbooks are written in, and katakana is far easier once the sound chart is already in your head. Katakana usually goes faster than hiragana did, because they're attaching new shapes to sounds they already know rather than starting from zero.

A practical way to practice:

1. **Get hiragana solid with recall, not recognition.** Cover the chart and write characters from their sounds. A [recall-first plan for memorizing hiragana](/how-to-memorize-hiragana/) works the same way for katakana later.
2. **Learn katakana by rows, starting with the look-alikes.** Spend your first session on the pairs that match hiragana (ヘ, カ, キ, セ, モ, ヤ, リ). It builds confidence quickly.
3. **Drill シ/ツ and ン/ソ by hand,** saying the direction aloud as you write: "up" or "down."
4. **Write real loanwords.** Copy words from a menu or a product label: コーヒー, ケーキ (*kēki*, cake), アイスクリーム (*aisukurīmu*, ice cream). You get the characters, the long-vowel dash and a bit of vocabulary all at once.
5. **Write your own name in katakana.** Look up the usual spelling (Emma is エマ, Daniel is ダニエル) and write it until it feels natural. It's the katakana word you'll use most.

Whatever tool you use, whether that's squared notebook paper or a handwriting app with stroke-order animations, the principle is the same. Writing makes you produce the shape and its direction yourself, and that's exactly what tells シ from ツ.

## Try this today

Take five minutes and a pen. Write シ, ツ, ン and ソ five times each, saying "up" or "down" on every final stroke. Then write your name in katakana once from memory. Tomorrow, before you look at anything, try those four again and see which ones stayed with you.
