Your child has colored a few loose Brawl Stars pages, and now they want more: a whole **Brawl Stars coloring book**, full of their favorite Brawlers, that they can work through from the first page to the last. It sounds like a simple request. Then you start looking and find a mix of listings, quality levels, and page counts, and it's hard to tell what's worth having.

Here's the useful reframe: what makes a coloring book special to a child isn't the printing. It's that a book is a **collection with an ending**. Loose pages get colored and forgotten. A book gets filled, and a filled book is something a kid keeps. Once you know that, you can pick a good one, or make one at home that's better than most you can buy.

A quick note: Brawl Stars is a game made by Supercell. Most Brawl Stars coloring material you'll find, printed or online, is **fan-made**, not official. That's fine for coloring at home, but it's worth knowing when you're paying for something.

## Why a book works differently from single pages

A stack of printouts and a bound book can contain the exact same drawings, yet children treat them differently.

1. **A book has progress you can see.** Flipping back through finished pages shows a child how far they've come, which is a strong reason to keep going.
2. **A book has an order.** Kids like working through something in sequence. "I'm on page twelve" is a goal; "I colored some stuff" isn't.
3. **A book is an object they own.** It gets carried around, shown to friends, and kept long after loose sheets have been recycled.
4. **A book shows growth.** The first pages are often scribbly and the last ones careful. Seeing that difference side by side is quiet proof that practice works.

That's also why a giant book can backfire. Two hundred pages is not a goal a seven-year-old can picture finishing. Something in the range of **15 to 30 pages** is long enough to feel like a real book and short enough to complete.

## If you're buying one: what to check

If you'd rather buy a printed book, a few minutes of checking saves disappointment.

- **Look at sample pages.** Good listings show interior pages. If they only show the cover, be cautious.
- **Check the line art.** Clean, closed outlines with large areas suit younger kids. Thin, sketchy lines and tiny details frustrate them.
- **Check single-sided printing.** Markers bleed through thin paper. Books printed on one side of each sheet hold up much better.
- **Read what reviewers say about the drawings.** Comments like "blurry," "pixelated," or "doesn't look like the characters" are common warning signs for quickly assembled books.
- **Count the characters, not just the pages.** A book that repeats the same few Brawlers gets boring fast. Variety matters more than page count.

## How to make your own Brawl Stars coloring book

A homemade book is often the better option, because your child helps choose what's in it, and that choice is half the fun. (If you need help finding and printing good line art, see [how to find and print Brawl Stars coloring pages](/brawl-stars-coloring-pages/).)

### 1. Let your child pick the lineup

Sit down together and ask them to list the Brawlers they most want to color. Their main, their friends' mains, the one they're saving up for. Aim for 15 to 20 characters. This list is the table of contents, and making it is already a conversation about something they love.

### 2. Order the pages from easy to hard

Put simple, bold pages at the front and more detailed ones toward the back. Early wins keep a child going, and the harder pages near the end feel like a challenge they've earned.

### 3. Leave room for their own ideas

Add a few blank or half-blank pages: "Design a new skin," "Draw your Brawler's base," "Invent a brand-new Brawler." These are often the pages kids care about most, because nothing in them was decided by anyone else.

### 4. Print single-sided on heavier paper

Use thicker paper or cardstock if you have it, especially if your child uses markers. Print in black and white so gray shading doesn't confuse younger kids about where to color.

### 5. Bind it simply

You don't need anything fancy. Any of these work:

- **Staples** along the left edge, for books up to about ten sheets.
- **A hole punch and ribbon or binder rings**, which lets pages lie flat and makes it easy to add more later.
- **A cheap folder or report cover**, for a book that feels "official" to a kid.

### 6. Let them make the cover

The cover is the most important page. Give them a blank sheet and let them title it, draw on it, and sign it as the "author." A book with their name on the front is a book they're proud of.

## Making it last longer than one afternoon

A coloring book works best as something they return to, not something they rush through.

- **One page a day is plenty.** Some kids like a small daily ritual: after school, before dinner, one Brawler.
- **Add a "done" date.** Writing the date in a corner of each finished page turns the book into a small record of their progress.
- **Talk about the choices.** Ask why they colored a character the way they did. You'll hear about skins, team strategies, and who's best, and they'll practice explaining something they know well. (There's more on why that matters in [what coloring actually does for a child's mind](/coloring-games-for-kids/).)
- **Celebrate the last page.** Finishing a whole book is a real accomplishment for a child. Mark it: a photo, a spot on the shelf, or the chance to plan "volume two."

Paper isn't the only option. For car rides or waiting rooms, a coloring app with game-style characters can do a similar job without crayons rolling under the seat. The same principle applies either way: a set of pages to work through, finished pieces they can look back on, and no timers or scores.

**Try it today:** ask your child to name the ten Brawlers they'd put in their own coloring book. Write the list down together. That's the table of contents, and by the weekend it can be a book with their name on the cover.
