You want to learn Japanese, but every beginner resource seems to offer a different starting point: memorize kanji, watch anime, buy a textbook, or download flashcards. If you're wondering **how to start learning Japanese**, the most useful first step is to choose a small sequence of skills rather than attempt everything at once.

A sensible beginning combines **listening, hiragana, useful phrases, and regular recall**. You can add more grammar, katakana, and kanji gradually. The goal of the first month is not fluency; it is to establish a routine that makes the next month easier.

## Start with sounds, not a giant vocabulary list

Japanese has five basic vowels, commonly represented as *a, i, u, e,* and *o*. Their pronunciation differs somewhat from familiar English approximations. Listen to recordings from a reliable beginner course and imitate short syllables rather than relying on romanized spellings alone.

Notice that Japanese rhythm is organized around morae, or timing units. For example, a long vowel takes more time than a short vowel. You do not need to master pitch accent immediately, but it is worth listening carefully from the beginning.

For the first few days, choose ten minutes of audio containing greetings and simple expressions. Pause, repeat, and compare what you said with the recording. A little attentive listening is more useful than leaving hours of unfamiliar speech playing in the background.

## Week 1: Learn the first hiragana and a few phrases

**Hiragana** is one of the Japanese writing systems and a practical starting point for reading beginner material. Instead of copying an entire chart repeatedly, learn a manageable group and test whether you can recognize and produce it without looking.

Begin with あ (*a*), い (*i*), う (*u*), え (*e*), and お (*o*). Then add another small row when you can recall the earlier one. Use a trustworthy stroke-order chart for handwriting; a text-only table does not show stroke direction.

Pair script practice with phrases you can actually use:

- こんにちは (*konnichiwa*) — hello
- ありがとうございます (*arigatō gozaimasu*) — thank you
- すみません (*sumimasen*) — excuse me / sorry
- はい (*hai*) — yes
- いいえ (*iie*) — no

These phrases are useful, but they are not interchangeable in every situation. Listen to examples of how speakers use them, rather than treating English translations as exact one-to-one matches.

For a focused approach to character recall, try [this hiragana memorization routine](/how-to-memorize-hiragana/).

## Week 2: Finish basic hiragana and build tiny sentences

Continue learning hiragana, including voiced sounds such as が (*ga*) and small ゃ, ゅ, and ょ combinations when you're ready. Learn how the small っ marks a doubled consonant in words such as きって (*kitte*, postage stamp). Use audio alongside written examples.

Now introduce a few sentence patterns. For instance:

- わたしは学生です。(*Watashi wa gakusei desu.*) — I am a student.
- これは本です。(*Kore wa hon desu.*) — This is a book.
- 水を飲みます。(*Mizu o nomimasu.*) — I drink water.

The particle は in the first sentence is written *ha* in the kana chart but pronounced *wa* when used as the topic marker. The object particle を is generally pronounced *o*. Learning these distinctions early prevents confusion later.

Avoid building sentences by translating English word by word. Japanese word order and particles work differently. A beginner textbook or structured course can explain why each example is formed the way it is.

## Week 3: Add katakana, reading, and deliberate listening

**Katakana** is commonly used for loanwords, many foreign names, and other special uses. Learn it after you have a basic foothold in hiragana, but keep reviewing both scripts.

Try reading familiar examples such as コーヒー (*kōhī*, coffee) and テレビ (*terebi*, television). Notice the long-vowel mark ー in コーヒー. The spelling may look recognizable, but the Japanese pronunciation is not identical to English.

Read a few sentences from a graded beginner resource. First listen without the text, then read along, then listen once more. Mark only a handful of unfamiliar words to review. Trying to record every unknown word can turn a short exercise into an exhausting research session.

If the two scripts still blur together, consult [the practical comparison of katakana and hiragana](/katakana-vs-hiragana/).

## Week 4: Make your first useful conversation

At this point, practice introducing yourself, asking a simple question, and answering it. You might prepare a short exchange about your name, interests, or what you like to eat.

Keep the language within what you have actually learned. A three-sentence conversation you understand is more useful than a polished paragraph you can only recite from memory.

Record yourself speaking, then compare with a clear model. Check whether your vowels, pauses, and long sounds are understandable. If you have access to a teacher or language partner, ask for corrections on one or two specific things instead of a general judgment of your Japanese.

You can also begin recognizing a few high-frequency kanji in context. Do not assume you must memorize hundreds of characters before you can speak or read simple kana-based material.

## A daily routine that fits into twenty minutes

Consistency is easier when each session has a clear end. Try this flexible plan:

1. **Five minutes:** listen and repeat a short dialogue.
2. **Five minutes:** review yesterday's kana or vocabulary without looking at answers.
3. **Five minutes:** study one new pattern and make two examples.
4. **Five minutes:** read, write, or say something from memory, then check it.

Writing can expose gaps that recognition hides, but copying is not the same as recall. For each new character, look at a model, cover it, write it, and check. For vocabulary, test both Japanese-to-meaning and meaning-to-Japanese directions.

On busy days, a five-minute review is enough to keep the routine alive. On days when you have more energy, spend the extra time listening to material you can mostly understand rather than racing through new lessons.

## How to tell whether you're making progress

Do not measure progress only by study streaks or the number of flashcards collected. Ask yourself concrete questions: Can you read yesterday's hiragana without a chart? Can you understand a short recorded greeting? Can you form a simple sentence without copying the example?

Keep a small weekly sample: five written words, a short voice recording, and one sentence you made yourself. Compare these over time. They show which skills are improving and which still need practice.

There is no single correct pace. Some learners will need several weeks just to become comfortable with kana; others will move faster because they already know another language or have more time. **A sustainable plan is better than an ambitious plan you abandon.**

To start today, listen to the five vowels, learn the first five hiragana, and practice one greeting aloud. Tomorrow, test what you remember before opening your notes.
