You learned hiragana, felt great about it, and then opened a page full of katakana. Same 46 sounds, completely different shapes, and somehow harder to hold onto. Characters that look like little sticks and corners slide out of memory, and シ and ツ look like the same smudge.

This is the most common wall in early Japanese, and it says nothing about your memory. **Katakana** is hard to memorize for specific reasons, and once you know them you can stop studying it the same way you studied hiragana.

## Why katakana is harder to keep than hiragana

Three things work against you.

1. **The shapes have less to grab onto.** Hiragana is curvy and varied, so each character has a distinctive outline. Katakana is mostly short straight strokes, so many characters differ by a single angle or the direction a stroke travels.
2. **You see it less.** In everyday text, hiragana fills every sentence. Katakana shows up mainly in loanwords, foreign names, sound effects and emphasis. Less exposure means fewer chances for a shape to sink in.
3. **It feels redundant.** Your brain already has a symbol for "ka." Learning a second one for the same sound feels like overwriting, and the two sets can get tangled at first.

The fix isn't more hours with a chart. It's to stop treating katakana as 46 new shapes and start using everything you already know.

## Step 1: Start with the characters you already half-know

Several katakana look a lot like their hiragana partners, because both were taken from the same kanji. Learn these first; they cost almost nothing:

- **か / カ** (ka): katakana drops the small extra stroke.
- **き / キ** (ki): katakana is the straight-lined version.
- **へ / ヘ** (he): practically identical.
- **り / リ** (ri): two strokes, just straighter.
- **せ / セ** (se), **も / モ** (mo), **や / ヤ** (ya): same skeleton, simpler lines.

Once these are in, you've cleared a good chunk of the chart before facing anything truly new, and you have a reference point for what "the katakana version" of a shape tends to look like.

## Step 2: Learn the look-alikes as groups, not one by one

Most katakana mistakes aren't forgetting a character entirely. They're mixing it up with its neighbor. So study the confusable ones side by side and name the difference out loud:

1. **シ (shi) / ツ (tsu).** In シ, the two short strokes sit on the left and the long stroke rises from the bottom. In ツ, the short strokes sit on top and the long stroke falls from the upper right.
2. **ン (n) / ソ (so).** The same logic: ン's long stroke rises, ソ's falls.
3. **ク (ku) / ケ (ke) / タ (ta).** All start with the same short slanted stroke. タ is ク with a dot inside; ケ has a horizontal and a long sweep instead of a hook.
4. **ウ (u) / ワ (wa) / フ (fu).** ウ has a dot and two sides, ワ has one vertical on the left, フ has none.
5. **コ (ko) / ユ (yu) / ロ (ro).** ユ's bottom line runs past the corner. ロ is a closed box.
6. **ル (ru) / レ (re).** レ is just the right half of ル.
7. **ノ (no) / メ (me).** メ is ノ with a short stroke cutting across it.

Writing a pair five times in a row, alternating, does more than staring at them on a chart. The direction of each stroke is often the only real difference, which is why [katakana stroke order](/katakana-stroke-order/) is worth getting right early rather than fixing later. For how the two scripts split the work in real sentences, see [katakana vs hiragana](/katakana-vs-hiragana/).

## Step 3: Learn katakana through words, not isolated characters

Here's the advantage katakana gives you: most of the words written in it are **loanwords**, often from English, so you already know what they mean. That makes them perfect practice material. Instead of memorizing ホ as an abstract shape, write it inside a word you can guess:

- **ホテル** (hoteru): hotel
- **タクシー** (takushī): taxi
- **コーヒー** (kōhī): coffee
- **カメラ** (kamera): camera
- **アイスクリーム** (aisukurīmu): ice cream
- **テレビ** (terebi): TV

Words like these also teach you katakana's three main conventions for adapting foreign sounds:

1. **The long-vowel bar ー** stretches the vowel before it: コーヒー, タクシー.
2. **A small ッ** doubles the next consonant: ベッド (beddo, bed).
3. **Small vowels** create sounds Japanese didn't originally have: パーティー (pātī, party), ファン (fan, fan).

Sounding out a menu or a label and realizing it says "sandwich" (サンドイッチ) is the moment katakana starts to stick, because the shapes now point to something you know.

## Step 4: Write from memory, every day, briefly

Recognition is the easy part. You can look at ヌ and eventually decide it's *nu*. **Recall** is what makes it permanent: hearing "nu" and producing ヌ yourself, with no model in front of you.

A simple daily loop:

1. **Write five characters from the chart** while saying each sound.
2. **Cover the chart** and write them again from memory.
3. **Check** shape, stroke direction and look-alikes. Circle any you hesitated on.
4. **Write two loanwords** that use today's characters.
5. **Start tomorrow with yesterday's circled ones.**

Ten minutes is enough. Short daily sessions spaced over a week or two tend to hold far better than one long cram, and the act of forming each stroke yourself gives your memory a second route besides sight. Plain squared paper works well, and so does a handwriting app that shows each stroke's direction and then asks you to write the character unaided. What matters is that you produce the character, not just look at it.

## Practical tips that make a real difference

- **Don't learn katakana before hiragana is solid.** Learning both at once is the main cause of the two sets blurring together.
- **Keep the order of the chart** (a, ka, sa, ta, na…). You already know it from hiragana, so it gives you a free scaffold.
- **Read katakana in the wild.** Menus, snack packaging, game screens and song titles are full of it. Each word you sound out counts as a review.
- **Expect a few stubborn ones.** For many learners it's シ/ツ, ソ/ン and ヌ. That's normal; keep them in your daily warm-up longer than the rest.

## A small step for today

Write カ, キ, ヘ, リ, セ, モ and ヤ next to their hiragana partners. Then cover the page and write them from memory. Finish by writing **コーヒー** once, slowly. That's seven characters, one word, and a real start.
