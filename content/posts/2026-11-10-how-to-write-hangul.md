If you can name a few Korean letters but still write them in a straight line, you're missing the most important part of **how to write Hangul**: Korean letters are grouped into **syllable blocks**. Each block represents one syllable. Once you understand where the pieces go, writing simple Korean words becomes much less mysterious.

You don't need beautiful handwriting to begin. You need to recognize the letters, place them correctly, and check what you've written.

## The basic rule: one block per syllable

English puts letters next to each other. Hangul groups letters into square-shaped units. A syllable usually begins with a consonant and includes a vowel; some syllables also have a final consonant.

Compare these examples:

| Letters | Written block | Sound (approximate) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ㄴ + ㅏ | 나 | na |
| ㅁ + ㅗ | 모 | mo |
| ㄱ + ㅏ + ㄴ | 간 | gan |
| ㅅ + ㅜ + ㄴ | 순 | sun |

The sound labels are only rough guides. Korean pronunciation does not always map neatly onto English letters.

The first thing to practice is **building a whole block**, not writing disconnected symbols across the page.

## Step 1: Put vertical vowels on the right

Some vowels, including **ㅏ, ㅓ, ㅣ, ㅐ, and ㅔ**, occupy the right side of the initial consonant.

Start with **ㄴ** and **ㅏ**. Write ㄴ on the left and ㅏ on the right, keeping both inside an imaginary square: **나**.

Now try:

- **가** = ㄱ + ㅏ
- **너** = ㄴ + ㅓ
- **미** = ㅁ + ㅣ
- **세** = ㅅ + ㅔ

The block's pieces can look narrower than the same letters written alone. That's normal: they must share a limited space.

**Practice:** Draw four squares. Write 가, 너, 미, 세 once while looking; then cover the examples and write them again. Check the vowel position, not just the overall appearance.

## Step 2: Put horizontal vowels underneath

Vowels such as **ㅗ, ㅜ, and ㅡ** sit below the initial consonant. This changes the shape of the block.

For **노**, put ㄴ above ㅗ. For **무**, put ㅁ above ㅜ. For **그**, put ㄱ above ㅡ.

| Top consonant | Lower vowel | Block |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ㄴ | ㅗ | 노 |
| ㅁ | ㅜ | 무 |
| ㄱ | ㅡ | 그 |

Don't stack letters so tightly that the strokes touch. Leave enough space to distinguish the top consonant from the vowel below.

Some compound vowels use both a lower and a right-hand area, as in **과** (ㄱ + ㅘ). Leave these until the simpler two-part layouts feel comfortable.

## Step 3: Add a final consonant at the bottom

A final consonant is called **받침 (batchim)**. It occupies the bottom of the syllable block, beneath the initial consonant-and-vowel combination.

Compare **가** with **간**. The second block adds ㄴ at the bottom:

- **가** = ㄱ + ㅏ
- **간** = ㄱ + ㅏ + ㄴ
- **밤** = ㅂ + ㅏ + ㅁ
- **손** = ㅅ + ㅗ + ㄴ
- **물** = ㅁ + ㅜ + ㄹ

Notice that the first two letters do not simply stay at their original full size. They make room for the final consonant.

Not every syllable has a batchim. Don't add one just to fill the square. Also, the sound of a final consonant can differ from its sound at the start of a syllable; spelling and pronunciation are related but not identical.

## Step 4: Remember the silent initial ㅇ

A Korean syllable that begins with a vowel still needs an initial consonant position in its written block. **ㅇ** fills that position without adding a consonant sound.

That is why **아** is written ㅇ + ㅏ, not simply ㅏ. Likewise:

- **오** = ㅇ + ㅗ
- **우** = ㅇ + ㅜ
- **이** = ㅇ + ㅣ

But ㅇ is **not silent when it is a final consonant**: in **강**, it represents an *ng* sound. Position matters.

Try writing 아, 오, 우, 이 from memory. Then write 강 and identify which ㅇ has a different role.

## Step 5: Join blocks to write whole words

A word is written as a sequence of syllable blocks. You still read from left to right, but you read each block as a unit.

For example:

- **나무** (*namu*, tree) = 나 + 무
- **바다** (*bada*, sea) = 바 + 다
- **사람** (*saram*, person) = 사 + 람
- **한국** (*Hanguk*, Korea) = 한 + 국

Start with two-block words. Say each syllable slowly, identify the consonant and vowel, and decide whether a final consonant is needed.

Do not rely on romanization to determine spelling forever. It is a temporary pronunciation aid, not a replacement for reading Hangul.

## Four mistakes to check before moving on

**1. Writing letters in a row.** ㄴㅏ is a useful way to show the components, but the finished syllable is **나**.

**2. Putting every vowel on the right.** The vowel in **노** goes below ㄴ; the vowel in **나** goes beside it.

**3. Forgetting ㅇ before an initial vowel.** Write **아**, not a standalone ㅏ, when you mean the syllable *a*.

**4. Squeezing a final consonant outside the block.** In **밤**, ㅁ belongs inside the same square as ㅂ and ㅏ.

If you're unsure whether a block is correct, separate it into its components, identify the vowel's orientation, and rebuild it.

## A ten-minute writing session

Use this short session to turn the layout rules into a habit:

1. **Two minutes:** write 나, 너, 미, 세 with right-side vowels.
2. **Two minutes:** write 노, 무, 그 with lower vowels.
3. **Two minutes:** write 간, 밤, 손, 물 with final consonants.
4. **Two minutes:** write 아, 오, 우, 이 with silent initial ㅇ.
5. **Two minutes:** cover your examples and write 나무, 바다, 사람, 한국 from memory.

Circle any block that looks wrong. Compare it with a reliable Hangul chart and rewrite only the parts you misplaced. That is more useful than filling a page with copies you never check.

## Start with one readable word

Knowing **how to write Hangul** is less about drawing perfect letters and more about understanding a consistent arrangement: consonant plus vowel, sometimes plus a final consonant. Today, write **나무** and **사람** without looking, then check every block. Two correctly assembled words are a solid beginning.
